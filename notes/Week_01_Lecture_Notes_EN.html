Week 01 · Start from scratch

Power: Separate what you have from what you change

01Main idea02Terms03Steps04Example05Practice

The main idea in a minute

Countries may have money, armies or popular films: resources. Understanding power requires another question: How did these resources change someone else’s thinking or behaviour? This week follows the path from possessing resources to achieving results.

Think of an everyday example

Imagine a hypothetical school club. A good hall is a resource; organising a film night is an activity; people showing up is attendance. Trusting the club and later joining are different results. A crowded hall does not prove changed minds. This distinction also helps explain countries’ communication.

What will you learn?

  • Describe the resource, activity and result in separate sentences.
  • Ask whether someone responded because of a reward, pressure or attraction.
  • Find missing evidence in a success claim and write a careful conclusion.

Start with these four terms

01Resource
Something an actor—a person, organisation or state—can use.
Example: A budget or respected university can be a resource; possessing it alone does not prove influence.
02Hard power
Influencing behaviour by changing another party's expected material gain or loss.
Example: Hypothetically, payment conditional on a decision is a material incentive; friendliness does not automatically make it soft power.
03Soft power
The capacity to shape preferences through attraction and being seen as justified and acceptable.
Example: Admiration for a university may be a resource; it does not show support for all its country's policies.
04Public diplomacy
Organised engagement with people in other countries, connected to external purposes. Partners outside government can participate.
Example: Educational exchanges may provide knowledge and relationships, without guaranteeing favourable opinions or shared policy support.

Understand it step by step

  1. 1
    Name the intended change

    Who wants whom to change, on which issue? Choose a specific target instead of “make the country more popular”. Is the aim greater knowledge, trust or a decision?

  2. 2
    Follow the path between them

    What happened, and how did people interpret it? This interpretation is called reception. Record attendance separately from what participants understood; audiences can interpret the same message differently.

  3. 3
    Consider other causes

    Could the observed change have happened without the programme? Previous interest or shared interests might also matter. Two events happening in sequence does not prove the first caused the second.

Let's work through an example

Worked example: A scholarship's effect

Hypothetical example: A country funds ten scholarships. Afterwards, eight students say they like the university. An administrator claims this created support for the country's foreign policy.

  1. Separate scholarships, an activity, from eight favourable university assessments. These measure different things.
  2. Look for students' initial views and separate answers about foreign policy. Liking a university does not mean approving that policy.
  3. Narrow the conclusion: These answers show a favourable assessment of the university; they do not yet show changed foreign-policy support.

Good analysis builds no success story bigger than its evidence.

A common mistake

More views mean more soft power.

What to remember

Views indicate potential reach. Audience interpretation, trust and preferences need other evidence.

Check your understanding

Does a hundred people attending a meeting mean a hundred were persuaded?

Show the answer and explanation

No. It shows attendance; persuasion requires evidence that views changed.

Now try it yourself

Examine what the Excel workbook’s hypothetical lab measures. For the scholarship example, add a claim, available evidence and one unknown to the student evidence matrix.

A hint to get started: Do not treat views, liking and policy support as one measure. Write 'unknown' for missing information, rather than zero.

Explain · examine · apply

A closer academic reading

This week examines power through relationships rather than possessions. Who can change which preference, under what conditions? We distinguish resources, instruments and outcomes, compare hard power, soft power and public diplomacy, then test success claims. The aim is to explain both knowledge and uncertainty.

01

Why possession does not establish achievement

Why this matters

Budgets and reputations describe resources; showing changed choices requires another investigation.

A resource is available capacity: money, expertise, military capability, reputation or a network. An instrument puts capacity to a purpose; a scholarship programme is one example. An outcome is an observed change in a target's knowledge, preference or behaviour. Separating these levels prevents us from assuming an attractive activity succeeded. Resources may remain unused, and instruments may receive unexpected responses.

Consider a hypothetical school club with an excellent hall. The hall is a resource, an introductory meeting is an instrument, and members voluntarily returning is a possible outcome. This analogy clarifies distinctions, but countries are more complex than clubs. States contain several institutions, objectives and time horizons. An activity can produce different responses among different publics; one group's favourable reaction cannot represent society.

Robert Dahl's relational approach asks whether one actor contributed to another doing something they otherwise would not have done. An actor may be a person, institution or state. The investigation therefore involves comparison: what might have happened without intervention? This counterfactual is usually unobservable directly. Nevertheless, examining prior preferences and alternative causes is stronger than inferring influence from superior resources.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A respected university

International reputation can attract students. A graduate's later support for a trade agreement does not alone prove university influence. Their employment, national interests and previous opinions also require investigation.

Pause and reason

What does an increased budget establish by itself?

Show the reasoning

An increase in available material resources. Reach, trust and behavioural change require separate observations.

02

Distinguish hard, soft and smart power through mechanisms

Why this matters

A mechanism explains how effects emerge. Names and friendly appearances cannot identify it.

Hard power changes another party's expected costs or material returns. Threats can make non-compliance expensive; conditional payments can make compliance rewarding. Hard power therefore extends beyond military violence. The result may be temporary behavioural compliance rather than genuine persuasion. Accepting a reward does not establish changed beliefs. Whether the behaviour continues after the reward disappears is a separate question.

Joseph Nye's soft power concerns preferences shaped through attraction and perceived legitimacy. Legitimacy here means regarding conduct as justified or acceptable. Culture, values and institutions may attract, but their meaning varies across audiences. Someone can enjoy a foreign film while opposing its country's foreign policy. The connection between admiration for a particular object and a particular policy preference therefore needs separate evidence.

Smart power is not a third substance alongside hard and soft power; it concerns combining instruments appropriately for purposes and conditions. Instruments can reinforce or undermine each other. Short-term pressure may secure compliance while weakening long-term trust. Evaluation should examine sequencing, interactions, audience interpretation and plausible alternatives, rather than counting how many instruments were employed. A mixed package is not automatically a coherent strategy.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: One scholarship, different reasons

One student applies for financial support, another from admiration for the institution. Their programme is identical, their reasons differ. Its cultural appearance cannot classify all participation as soft power.

Pause and reason

Why is a positive incentive not always soft power?

Show the reasoning

Behaviour may respond to conditional material gain rather than attraction. Classification depends on why the target responded.

03

Decisions, agendas and preferences raise different questions

Why this matters

Open votes can miss power; explaining every silence through power makes claims untestable.

Power's most visible face involves explicit disagreement and a decision. Who wanted what, which options were discussed, and what happened? Voting records and negotiation documents help here. Yet the winner's greater resources do not establish that those resources produced the outcome. The other party might have preferred the same option for its own reasons. Investigate the reasoning behind the choice.

The framework expanded by Steven Lukes also asks about influence over agendas: which issues never entered authoritative discussion? An absent item alone is insufficient. Investigate rejected applications, rules excluding particular groups or repeated patterns of obstruction. Ignorance, insufficient time and administrative routines can also explain absence. Researchers should compare these alternatives before making a firm claim about control.

A third question concerns how people come to regard things as natural, possible or desirable. Education, relationships and narratives can shape options' meanings. Treating all learning as domination would nevertheless be mistaken: independent assessment can also change opinions. Claims should trace a particular preference's development, interactions and alternatives over time. These three perspectives may coexist within one case, but require different evidence.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A club's meeting agenda

Rejecting a proposal in a vote concerns a decision. Rules preventing its discussion are different. Members considering it unthinkable raises a preference-formation question. These observations cannot substitute for one explanation.

Pause and reason

Does an absent issue in the minutes prove agenda control?

Show the reasoning

No. Investigate how it remained absent, comparing ignorance, time constraints and routine as possible explanations.

04

Public diplomacy identifies a practice, not proven success

Why this matters

Definitions of practice do not establish effects or automatically validate cultural activities.

Public diplomacy is organised engagement with foreign publics connected to external purposes. States usually undertake or enable it, while journalists, universities and civic organisations may participate. It remains connected to conventional diplomacy, but its primary interlocutors extend beyond government representatives. Do not imagine a public as one uniform community: students, specialists and social groups differ in access to information, interests and connections to decisions.

Nicholas Cull's functional distinctions help separate listening, advocacy, cultural diplomacy, exchange and international broadcasting. A function describes what an activity does; a mechanism explains how it might change something. Producing a listening report establishes information collection. If that report changes programme design or policy advice, organisational learning becomes a defensible claim. One programme can fulfil several functions, each requiring appropriate indicators.

Propaganda and public diplomacy sometimes use identical communication channels. Tone or medium alone therefore cannot settle their boundary. Assess source transparency, truthfulness, selectivity, opportunities to object and attention to feedback. Participation by a non-governmental partner does not itself establish independence. Investigate authority, funding, control over content and the implementer's discretion separately. Naming the practice does not resolve questions about its quality.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Opinions collected but unused

A cultural centre collects visitor suggestions but changes nothing. Listening activity may be established; learning or mutual adaptation is not. Records of changes would better support that second claim.

Pause and reason

What does calling something public diplomacy not prove?

Show the reasoning

That it is effective, ethical, reciprocal or persuasive. These require separate assessment.

05

Separate evidence from the conclusion you draw

Why this matters

No document answers everything. Identify its strengths and limits.

Evidence is an observation used to assess a claim; inference is the conclusion drawn from it. An activity record can establish a meeting occurred, without establishing increased trust. Ask who produced a source, for what purpose, for whom and when. An organisation's success report is not worthless, but may favour positive self-presentation. A participant's account may not represent everyone who attended.

Reception means how people interpret messages they encounter. Distribution and views provide clues about reach; interpretation, recall and trust require different evidence. Preferences and behaviour introduce further questions. These stages are connected, but no stage automatically proves the next. Search for misunderstanding, rejection and unexpected uses alongside favourable responses. Otherwise, the analysis risks constructing its explanation entirely from selected success stories.

Comparing sources can identify independent traces of the same claim. But three reports repeating one press release are not three independent confirmations. Check whether sources derive from each other. Missing documents do not invariably mean missing effects; records may be incomplete. In such situations, identify the observed stage and remaining uncertainty instead of announcing definite success or failure.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Three stories, one source

Three newspapers reproduce the same institutional announcement. Their number can indicate circulation, but not three independent favourable assessments. A reporter's investigation and repetition of a press statement carry different evidential weight.

Pause and reason

How broad a claim can one favourable participant account support?

Show the reasoning

Primarily that person's reported assessment. Generalising to an entire audience or a government's preferences requires additional evidence.

06

Build a proportionate causal and ethical assessment

Why this matters

Explain contributions while retaining other causes and conditions of interaction.

A causal claim proposes that something contributed to something else's occurrence. Temporal order is necessary but insufficient. Examine previous attitudes, participant selection and concurrent developments. If already interested people disproportionately joined, favourable attitudes afterwards may not originate entirely in the programme. This selection problem can also distort a simple comparison between participants and non-participants: the groups may have differed before any intervention.

Evaluation needs measures appropriate to objectives. Accurate recall may suit a short informational broadcast; trust and sustained contact may better suit a relationship programme. Requiring every small activity to demonstrate a distant government decision can also be unfair. Define the intended contribution, then establish which part the evidence supports. Verbs such as 'facilitated' or 'reinforced' may fit limited process evidence more accurately.

Ethical conditions are not outside the mechanism. Concealed sponsorship, misuse of personal information or damaged partner independence can change trust. Transparency, opportunities to object and reciprocity may support credibility without guaranteeing success. When transferring a lesson, identify comparable mechanisms and different conditions rather than copying an instrument. Historical analogy helps by sharpening research questions, not by erasing differences between situations.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Missing baseline

Favourable responses exist after a programme, but initial opinions are unknown. Rather than claiming improved attitudes, report favourable final assessments and acknowledge that the source of change remains undetermined.

Pause and reason

Does acknowledging uncertainty weaken analysis?

Show the reasoning

No. Explaining its cause and the evidence needed to reduce it defines the claim's limits and the next research step.

Case workshop

Case workshop 1

Hypothetical case: Scholarships and foreign-policy support

Graduates praise a university. An administrator infers increased support for its country's foreign policy.

  1. Separate university and foreign-policy assessments. Combining them as positive attitudes loses what was actually measured.
  2. Investigate initial views and selection. People already attracted to the country may have joined, providing an alternative explanation.
  3. Seek separate policy questions and behavioural records. Without them, report university assessments, not broader policy effects.
A defensible conclusion

The evidence may support a positive experience; a more distant diplomatic outcome remains unproven.

Case workshop 2

Hypothetical case: Successful correction or distribution alone?

An institution sees its correction shared by many accounts and concludes that the false belief disappeared.

  1. Classify sharing as an indicator of reach. Sharing may express support, criticism or ridicule; examine the accompanying language.
  2. Investigate initial exposure to false information. Without knowing who received the correction, its scope remains unknown.
  3. Examine later recall and reasoning. If another source corrected the claim simultaneously, attempt to distinguish their contributions.
A defensible conclusion

Publishing, seeing and believing a correction are three separate claims. Keep their evidence separate.

Research practice

Narrowing a claim with Excel

Use the student evidence matrix to assess the scholarship case. Preserve the hypothetical lab's status as example data; do not present it as real student results.

  1. Enter the claim and intended outcome. Explicitly distinguish university satisfaction, trust and policy support.
  2. For every observation, record its source type and the stage it establishes. Mark missing information unknown, not zero.
  3. Add the strongest alternative explanation: could prior interest, selection or another event explain the same finding?
  4. Write a narrower conclusion and the next evidence needed. If using colour, do not equate available evidence with a confirmed claim.
Read a model response

Example: Graduates report favourable university assessments. Unknown initial views and policy preferences prevent attributing change to the programme. Next, compare topic-specific initial and final assessments for the same participants and investigate selection.

Questions worth asking

Is every cultural activity soft power?

No. A cultural instrument may attract, encounter indifference or function as a material opportunity. A soft-power claim requires evidence of attraction's role in the target's preferences.

Does no visible outcome mean failure?

Not necessarily. The purpose may be informational, observation too brief or records incomplete. Assess the objective and measurement first. Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of ineffectiveness.

Must I choose one explanation?

Several mechanisms may contribute. Specify conditions, supporting observations and unexplained elements instead of merely listing causes.

The academic argument

Power: Separate what you have from what you change

This week clarifies what power is and how different forms of power produce outcomes through distinct mechanisms, providing the conceptual foundation needed to understand public diplomacy.

The analytical problem: Resource, relationship and outcome

Week One begins with a problem of inference: analysts often treat assets as if they were power itself. Armed forces, finance, technology, diplomatic access, cultural prestige and institutional credibility are resources that may create capacity, but capacity is not yet influence. A resource matters only when an actor can mobilise it through an instrument in a particular relationship and under particular conditions. Its relevance therefore varies by objective, audience and setting. A large military establishment may deter one opponent yet prove irrelevant to a dispersed public; a respected university may open doors yet fail to change a government’s choice. Inventory measures are useful evidence of potential, but they cannot establish either the mechanism or the outcome of power. Study guide: Make the distinction concrete with a causal table. Put the actor's resource in the first column, the instrument in the second, the incentive or change in meaning experienced by the target in the third, and the observed outcome in the last. A prestigious university is a resource; a scholarship is an instrument; greater trust or curiosity is a possible intermediate mechanism; a graduate's later support for cooperation is a separate outcome that still requires explanation. Then ask whether the same preference would have appeared without the programme. This counterfactual question may not yield certainty, but it exposes rival explanations such as shared interest, prior affinity or pressure from a third party. Dahl directs attention to observable relational change, whereas Lukes also opens excluded issues and the construction of preferences to investigation. Treat them as lenses for different evidential problems, not as names to recite. A strong analysis specifies the target, mechanism, timing and direction of change instead of merely listing the stronger actor's assets.

Relational approaches, associated with Robert Dahl, ask whether actor A contributes to actor B doing something B would not otherwise have done. This directs attention from possession to interaction: who seeks what change, which options the target perceives, how resistance occurs and what decision follows. Evidence might include altered choices after a credible threat, acceptance of an inducement, or reasoning recorded in a decision process. Yet observed sequence is not enough. B may already have intended to act, may respond to a third party, or may change because circumstances shifted. The counterfactual is rarely observable directly, so claims should be calibrated to the quality of process evidence rather than inferred from correlation or from A’s superior resources. Checkpoint: Reduce this section to four lines: context, the actor's objective, the proposed mechanism and the outcome requiring proof. Add one rival explanation. The exercise separates chronological storytelling from a causal claim and shows which link still needs evidence.

Steven Lukes broadens analysis beyond visible contests. One dimension concerns decisions in open conflict; a second concerns agenda control, whereby some issues are excluded from authoritative consideration; a third concerns the formation of preferences and perceived possibilities. These dimensions identify different mechanisms and therefore require different evidence: voting or bargaining records for overt decisions, patterns of exclusion for agenda-setting, and credible accounts of socialisation or framing for preference formation. The breadth of the framework is also its danger. If every silence or preference is labelled power, the claim becomes difficult to test. Researchers should specify whose alternatives were narrowed, through what process, and consider ignorance, convention, institutional routine or autonomous learning as rival explanations.

Actors and institutions: Hard, soft and smart power

Hard power operates principally by changing the target’s expected costs or material benefits. Military threats, sanctions and coercive diplomacy can raise the price of non-compliance, while payments, aid, market access or security guarantees can make compliance more rewarding. Positive inducements may appear friendlier than threats, but they remain analytically distinct from attraction when the operative mechanism is a conditional material exchange. The immediate outcome may be behavioural compliance without agreement, trust or durable preference change. Evidence must therefore identify the offer or pressure, its credibility, the target’s calculation and the resulting choice. Similar behaviour might instead reflect shared interests or domestic necessity; visible compliance can also conceal delay, evasion, resentment or preparations to resist later. Study guide: Classify hard, soft and smart power by the change in the target's decision process rather than by memorised lists of instruments. A scholarship may induce behaviour as a material benefit, generate attraction through institutional reputation and create a durable network; several mechanisms can coexist in one programme. The classification turns on why the target responded, not on whether the object looked cultural or military. Smart-power analysis likewise asks whether instruments reinforced one another, not simply how many were used. Coercion that secures immediate compliance but damages credibility can weaken a later exchange programme. A credible security guarantee, by contrast, might create space for dialogue. Identify the objective, audience, time horizon, possible backlash and exit conditions. This prevents two common errors: treating every agreeable instrument as soft power and treating every mixed portfolio as smart. It also clarifies that effectiveness and ethical legitimacy are related questions but not interchangeable answers.

Soft power, in Joseph Nye’s formulation, concerns the capacity to shape preferences through attraction and perceived legitimacy rather than coercion or payment. Culture, political values, institutions, expertise and the conduct of foreign policy may supply relevant resources, but none is intrinsically or universally attractive. Audiences interpret them through local histories, identities and current experience; admiration for a film, university or social practice need not extend to support for the sponsoring state’s policy. A defensible soft-power claim traces a pathway from exposure to favourable evaluation, from evaluation to altered preference and, where alleged, from preference to behaviour. Even then, affinity may pre-date the programme, while disliked policies, unequal relationships or competing narratives can interrupt the proposed mechanism. Checkpoint: Do not treat actors as a bloc. Record authority, resources, audience, interest and autonomy for each. Similar language is not proof of coordination; look for instructions, shared finance, regular information exchange or a documented division of labour.

Smart power is a strategic principle, not a third substance added to hard and soft power. It means diagnosing the objective and audience, then sequencing or combining instruments whose mechanisms are mutually supportive. Security protection might create space for educational exchange; exchange may improve understanding without securing agreement; coercion may achieve an urgent decision while weakening later credibility. Institutional co-ordination matters because defence, foreign affairs, development, broadcasting and cultural bodies can send inconsistent signals or pursue different time horizons. A mixture is not automatically smart: piling communication onto a poorly designed policy can amplify its defects. Evaluation should ask whether the combination addressed the target’s constraints more effectively and with fewer counterproductive effects than plausible alternatives.

Instruments and operations: Public diplomacy and foreign public engagement

Public diplomacy is the organised engagement of foreign publics in support of external purposes, usually undertaken or enabled by a state but often involving partners beyond government. It differs from traditional diplomacy in primary interlocutors rather than in complete separation: ministries and embassies still matter, yet students, journalists, specialists, cultural organisations, diasporas, businesses and civic groups may become audiences, intermediaries or co-producers. Governments do not fully control these actors, and shared participation does not demonstrate shared motives. Persuasion seeks to change an attitude or preference through reasons and evidence, whereas attraction rests on an actor’s values, culture or conduct being regarded as desirable. Propaganda is systematic communication intended to direct perception and behaviour towards a political purpose. Because propaganda and public diplomacy can use the same media, their boundary should be assessed through purpose, source transparency, truthfulness and selectivity, audience agency, reciprocity and feedback rather than medium alone. The analytical task is to identify authority, funding, implementation and room for independent action. Calling an activity public diplomacy describes a field of engagement; it does not establish that the activity was dialogic, ethical, persuasive or effective. Study guide: Begin an assessment of public diplomacy with an actor–public–channel map. Who supplies funds, who produces content, who translates it into local language and context, who is addressed, and who receives feedback? These questions prevent the formal sponsor from being confused with an independent implementer. Next distinguish informing, advocacy, relationship-building, exchange and listening. One programme can perform several functions, but each needs a separate indicator of success. Publication totals show production, views show potential reach, audience research can illuminate reception, and a documented programme revision indicates organisational learning. When comparing public diplomacy with propaganda, do not rely on tone alone. Source transparency, responsiveness to correction, audience autonomy and the institutional consequences of feedback are more revealing. In historical cases, record contemporary terminology and authority rather than projecting today's agency chart backwards. This approach keeps an analytical category useful without turning every international contact into evidence of a centrally directed campaign.

Operational instruments include policy explanation and advocacy, international broadcasting, digital communication, cultural presentation, educational exchange, professional networks and listening. These labels do not determine the mechanism. A broadcast may provide credible information, prime an agenda or merely signal presence; an exchange may transfer knowledge, generate interpersonal trust or function chiefly as a funded opportunity. Listening becomes consequential only if information from foreign publics enters organisational learning or changes communication, programme design or policy advice. One-way transmission may sometimes meet a limited informational purpose, whereas reciprocity may be essential for a long-term relationship. Instrument choice should therefore follow the intended intermediate change, not a presumption that any cultural or communicative activity automatically produces soft power. Checkpoint: Separate instrument from mechanism. A newspaper, meeting, relief package or exchange is an instrument; information, cost, trust or identification is a possible mechanism. Because one instrument can produce opposed responses, specify audience interpretation and contextual conditions.

Caitlin E. Schindler’s concept of foreign public engagement offers a broader historical lens for practices that preceded the institutional label of public diplomacy. It permits analysis of diplomats, newspapers, voluntary associations, commercial actors and individuals whose activities connected a state or society with publics abroad. The category is useful precisely because formal control may be partial and institutional boundaries may be fluid. It should not, however, turn every cross-border contact into public diplomacy or project contemporary agencies backwards in time. Researchers should establish an external political relevance, an identifiable channel of engagement and the relationship, if any, to official objectives. Parallel activity is not proof of co-ordination, and analytical resemblance is not institutional identity.

Reception, decisions and rival explanations

Foreign publics are not empty vessels into which messages are placed. They select, translate, discuss, resist and repurpose communication through existing beliefs, media systems and social relationships. The sender’s intention therefore cannot stand in for reception. Local journalists, alumni, teachers or civic organisations may lend credibility and contextual knowledge, but they can also modify the message or reject its premises. Evidence of reception includes audience accounts, discussion, questions, recall, interpretation and changes in trust, not merely distribution totals, attendance or online impressions. Even favourable reception remains an intermediate outcome: understanding is not endorsement, endorsement is not behaviour, and behaviour by some participants is not necessarily a wider shift in public opinion or a government decision. Study guide: Use a simple logic model for evaluation: inputs, activities, outputs, immediate reception, intermediate preferences and final decisions. Money and staff are inputs; a meeting is an activity; attendance and publications are outputs; accurate recall is reception; a change in trust or option ranking is an intermediate outcome; a vote, agreement or behaviour is more distant. No rung proves the next automatically. A campaign can reach millions yet be misunderstood, while a small specialist network can matter because it connects to a decision channel. Look for negative cases as well as favourable testimony: did change occur where exposure was weak, or fail where exposure was strong? Audience accounts, behaviour records and internal decision documents carry different biases and should be triangulated where possible. In the conclusion, choose verbs that fit the evidence. ‘Enabled’, ‘reinforced’ or ‘kept on the agenda’ is often more defensible than ‘caused’, and it tells the reader precisely what contribution is being claimed.

A disciplined evaluation separates five stages. Intention records what an actor hoped to change; activity records what it funded, produced or delivered; reception records how specified publics encountered and interpreted it; decision evidence shows whether those interpretations entered an individual, organisational or governmental choice; outcome evidence identifies the change that followed. Each transition is a claim requiring support. Strategy papers can establish purpose but not reception; programme records establish implementation but not persuasion; surveys or interviews may reveal attitudes but not their cause; internal deliberations can illuminate decisions but may be incomplete or self-serving. The further the claim travels towards policy impact, the more necessary it becomes to combine sources and test the missing links. Checkpoint: Mark each step in reception: access, attention, comprehension, acceptance, preference and behaviour. Stop where the evidence stops. Silence is not approval, circulation is not persuasion, elite endorsement is not general opinion, and sequence alone is not causation.

Rival explanations are central rather than an afterthought. A favourable decision attributed to public diplomacy may also reflect material interest, alliance pressure, leadership change, domestic mobilisation, battlefield events, economic conditions or a competitor’s mistake. Timing can rule out some accounts, while comparisons across audiences, documents from decision-makers and evidence of message uptake can strengthen others. Researchers may also ask whether the outcome occurred where exposure was weak, or failed where exposure was strong. Such comparisons rarely deliver experimental certainty in historical cases, but they discipline attribution. The appropriate conclusion may be that engagement facilitated access, reinforced an existing tendency or increased the political usability of a choice, rather than that it independently caused the final outcome.

Synthesis: Evidence, limits and transfer

The concepts can be joined in a mechanism map: actor and objective; relevant resource; instrument and intermediary; target public; hard, soft or mixed mechanism; intended intermediate change; decision channel; observable outcome; necessary conditions; and possible backlash. Consider a scholarship. Funding is a material resource and access to it an inducement, while education, relationships and institutional reputation may generate attraction or trust. Neither mechanism follows automatically from the programme’s existence. Selection rules, participant experience, alumni networks, political context and later opportunities shape what travels beyond the award. The map prevents an attractive resource, a visible activity and a distant diplomatic outcome from being collapsed into a single assertion of success. Study guide: Synthesize the week through one bounded research question: under what conditions did a specified engagement alter a specified preference among a specified public? This formulation identifies actor, target, outcome and setting. Begin research with documents showing official intention, then implementation records, independent traces of reception and evidence from the decision process. Note what each source cannot establish: an institutional report can verify that an event occurred, for example, but not that participants were persuaded. Ethics should not be an afterthought. Source transparency, protection of personal data, the safety of local partners and reciprocity affect credibility and therefore the causal mechanism itself. When transferring a historical lesson, identify which mechanism remains comparable and which features of the media system or political environment have changed. The point is not that an old instrument will work again, but that a carefully specified pathway can be tested in a new setting. This turns analogy into disciplined comparison rather than a slogan.

Evidence should be matched to the stage and claim. Administrative records clarify resources and activity; media or network analysis can show circulation; interviews, audience research and participant testimony illuminate reception; internal correspondence and minutes may reveal whether perceptions entered a decision; behavioural or policy records establish what changed. Triangulation matters because each source has characteristic limits, including official self-promotion, respondent recall, selective archives and indicators chosen for convenience. Researchers should record negative and unintended effects as well as favourable ones, and distinguish documented fact, plausible inference and unresolved question. Ethical evaluation belongs beside causal evaluation: transparency, consent, reciprocity and risk to local partners affect legitimacy and may themselves alter the mechanism. Checkpoint: State the main claim in one sentence and its evidential limit in another. Then identify a document that could change your judgment, such as a decision record, an opponent's letter, circulation evidence or a failed comparison case.

Three cautions are transferable across the course. First, hard and soft power are mechanisms, not moral rankings: inducement may be benign, attraction may be manipulated, and either may produce resistance. Secondly, public diplomacy is not simply soft power in action; it can explain coercive policy, accompany material incentives or create knowledge without changing preferences. Thirdly, foreign public engagement is a historical and analytical frame, not proof of a centrally directed programme. Strong analysis therefore moves in modest steps from intention to activity, reception, decision and outcome, naming alternative causes at each point. Its most credible conclusion is often conditional: a resource contributed through a specified mechanism under identifiable circumstances, within limits that the available evidence can sustain.

Under what observable conditions does communication directed at a foreign public move beyond reach and become soft power that changes a foreign policy preference?

Source criticism

Read Robert A. Dahl's ‘The Concept of Power’ (1957) as an original theoretical intervention: extract the logic by which a claim of influence requires an actor, a preference, and a counterfactual. This work is not primary evidence about a historical case; it supplies a criterion to apply to next week's documents.

Seminar task

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, compare Schindler's historical framework with Dahl's relational criterion of power. For one example, specify four distinct observations concerning instrument, reach, reception, and preference change. Explain how you would test the rival account that increased visibility alone constitutes influence, and identify the point at which the available sources cease to suffice.

Synthesis

  1. Power should be examined separately at the levels of resources, relationships and outcomes.
  2. Hard power operates through costs and incentives, whereas soft power relies on attraction and legitimacy.
  3. Cultural visibility or exposure to a message does not, by itself, demonstrate influence.
  4. Public diplomacy encompasses listening, reciprocity and relationship-building as well as speaking.
  5. Schindler’s foreign public engagement framework makes it possible to examine practices that pre-dated modern terminology.

Questions for discussion

  1. Under what conditions can a scholarship programme be considered soft power, and under what conditions is it a material incentive?
  2. Which criteria should be used to distinguish propaganda from public diplomacy?
  3. How can we demonstrate that a communication campaign genuinely changed behaviour?
