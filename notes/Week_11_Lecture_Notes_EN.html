Week 11 · Start from scratch

The film was screened: What did people think?

01Main idea02Terms03Steps04Example05Practice

The main idea in a minute

Producing a message, delivering it and changing minds are different things. This week follows radio, film and press. Under the Good Neighbor Policy, the United States promoted friendship and non-intervention in Latin America. Yet distrust arising from earlier interventions did not automatically disappear with a new message.

Think of an everyday example

Imagine a hypothetical class event. Writing an invitation, sending it, friends reading it and their attendance are separate stages. A 'sent' notification does not even prove everyone read the message. In public diplomacy, we likewise avoid jumping from 'published' to 'persuaded'.

What will you learn?

  • Explain the different access routes for radio, film and press.
  • Choose separate evidence for production, exposure and reception.
  • Propose another explanation for a campaign's apparent effect.

Start with these four terms

01Potential reach
Content is available to someone; this does not establish actual viewing or listening.
Example: Hypothetical: A radio signal reaches a city, but not everyone owns a receiver.
02Exposure
Someone actually encounters content. Attention and belief require further investigation.
Example: Hypothetical: Cinema records show attendance at a particular screening.
03Reception
How people interpret, enjoy, criticise or reject a message.
Example: Hypothetical: A viewer's letter praises a film but rejects its political conclusion.
04Local intermediary
An editor, translator or distributor who shapes selection, language or circulation.
Example: Hypothetical: A newspaper editor changes the supplied headline.

Understand it step by step

  1. 1
    Match medium to possibility

    Radio can provide rapid, repeated broadcasts; film combines images and narrative; press offers detailed text. These are technical possibilities. Check practical steps: language and receivers for radio, cinemas for film, editorial decisions for press.

  2. 2
    Find local interpretation

    Read beyond the producer's success report. Seek local newspapers, viewers' letters or listening records. One letter shows one person's response, not a country's opinion. Three newspapers copying one agency story are not three independent observations.

  3. 3
    Compare other explanations

    Government cooperation may follow a film, but trade, security or domestic politics could also matter. Examine decision-makers' reasons and timing. Require separate findings for enjoying the film, trusting its source and supporting its policy.

Let's work through an example

Worked exercise: What do ten screenings show?

An entirely hypothetical file contains ten screenings, 200 recorded attendances and five favourable viewers' letters. How the letter writers were selected is unknown.

  1. Record ten screenings as activity. Do not automatically count 200 attendances as 200 different people; repeat visits are possible.
  2. Record five letters as five favourable responses. Do not assume volunteer writers represent all viewers.
  3. State that policy support is unknown. Do not divide five letters by 200 and declare a public-support rate.

The file can document screenings and some favourable interpretations. It establishes neither universal persuasion nor a changed political decision.

A common mistake

Diplomatic influence increases in direct proportion to audience size.

What to remember

More opportunities for contact do not mean greater trust. Interpretations differ, and negative reactions are possible.

Check your understanding

Does an archive catalogue listing prove foreign audiences watched a film?

Show the answer and explanation

No. A listing may indicate production or preservation; screenings and viewing in a particular place need separate documentation.

A hint to get started: Missing responses do not mean zero effect. Unknown and zero differ.

Explain · examine · apply

A closer academic reading

Producing a film, screening it abroad and changing viewers’ minds are different events. This week examines radio, film and press as processes operated by people and institutions, rather than magical instruments of influence. The Good Neighbor Policy provides a setting in which friendship messages encountered earlier experience. Our objective is to identify what each stage establishes and which conclusions still require evidence.

Read in stages

01

A visibility gap differs from a trust problem

Why this matters

More messages do not automatically overcome doubts arising from earlier conduct.

The supplied materials place American communication in Latin America within histories of intervention, economic relationships and inequality. Defining the problem simply as insufficient knowledge about America is misleading. People can know the sender and distrust it. The required change then differs from increasing information volume. Diagnosing the problem shapes both the instrument selected and the criteria used to assess success.

Franklin D. Roosevelt’s Good Neighbor Policy is associated with non-intervention and mutual-respect language. Radio, film and press could make this approach visible, but consistency between statements and conduct requires separate assessment. One announcement cannot establish an entire region’s experience. Country, date and social group matter. Communication may describe a policy change without having caused the change itself.

Two interpretations create useful tension. One highlights opportunities for cultural understanding; another stresses a powerful state’s strategic interests. A programme can contain both. Rather than reducing a film to sincere friendship or concealed manipulation, compare content, funding, local decisions and audience interpretation. Examining purpose, process and outcome supports a bounded explanation instead of an assumed moral or causal verdict.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical analogy: An unreliable neighbour

A neighbour who previously broke promises sends a friendship letter. The letter supplies information, but trust may also require changed behaviour. The analogy explains consistency without reducing international inequality to a personal relationship.

Pause and reason

Does low trust always indicate missing information?

Show the reasoning

No. People may understand policy and distrust it because of experience or interests.

02

Separate technical possibility from observed effect

Why this matters

What a medium makes possible differs from what it accomplishes in a particular setting.

Radio enables rapid, repeated audio communication. Transmitter coverage is not evidence of listening. Receiver ownership, signal quality, scheduling and language condition the passage from potential access to actual encounter. A signal reaching a city does not establish equal access across its social groups. Keeping broadcast logs separate from audience evidence is therefore a basic methodological decision.

Film combines narrative, image and sound in a potentially intense shared experience. That possibility is not proof of persuasion. Preparing, translating, shipping, approving and scheduling a print involve separate decisions. Viewers may then interpret it differently. Screening records document exhibition in a place; who attended, paid attention and formed which judgement require additional sources.

Print can provide detailed explanation and permit rereading. Literacy, paper, distribution and editorial choices nevertheless constrain access. Supplied material may never appear, or appear shortened beneath a critical headline. Match each medium to audience conditions instead of ranking them universally. Different affordances generate expectations to test, not an automatic hierarchy of diplomatic effectiveness.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: One story, different access routes

A story is broadcast and sent to a newspaper. A broadcast log confirms the first activity; newspaper receipt does not establish publication. Using one access indicator for both confuses different processes.

Pause and reason

What does a signal reaching an area establish?

Show the reasoning

It supports technical availability; listening, trust and persuasion require separate evidence.

03

Intermediaries transform messages as well as carry them

Why this matters

Decision makers between producers and audiences belong at the centre of the explanation.

Translators, editors, distributors, cinema operators and local partners influence a message’s route. Treating them as pipes erases their selection powers. They can shorten text, remove images, change headlines or refuse publication. Comparing central and local versions makes mediation observable. Audiences in different places may encounter different content under the same campaign name.

Private partnerships can supply established distribution and expertise, but public and commercial purposes are not identical. Firms may protect audience appeal, editors their publication’s position, and local partners their political standing. Do not automatically label these differences sabotage; examine them as operating conditions. Contracts show formal obligations, correspondence practical negotiations, and published versions final content.

Local adaptation can improve comprehension or introduce new stereotypes. There may be no universally correct balance between fidelity and local relevance. Investigate what changed, why and how audiences responded. Do not equate an intermediary’s view with the whole society’s voice. Its social reach, economic relationships and political connections define the limits of its representative claim.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A changed headline

A supplied friendship article appears under a sceptical local headline. Circulation occurred, but framing changed. The document shows editorial intervention, not that every reader shared the editor’s doubts.

Pause and reason

Is a local headline change direct evidence of public opinion?

Show the reasoning

It is first an editorial decision. Whether it represents readers’ responses requires investigation.

04

Each source illuminates a different link

Why this matters

The best-documented stage is not necessarily the stage with the strongest effects.

A production order concerns intention and activity; a completed copy establishes output; shipment lists concern distribution; technical logs address availability. Attendance records approach actual exposure, while letters and reviews reveal particular interpretations. Decision minutes may illuminate behavioural or political connections. These sources are not interchangeable. Identify the supporting record and the missing link whenever moving between stages.

Limited or interested sources are not worthless. Agencies may select successes, letter writers select themselves, and diplomats observe accessible circles. Narrow the question accordingly. A letter demonstrates an interpretation’s existence rather than prevalence. Favourable correspondence cannot become an opinion percentage without a defensible population and sample. The source’s social coverage must bound the conclusion’s scope.

Interpret absence cautiously. An event might not have occurred, might not have been recorded, or might have lost its records. Absence from an otherwise comprehensive screening register can be informative; absence from randomly surviving files may say little. Investigate record creation and preservation first. Unknown is not a numerical finding of zero influence or definite failure.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A silent archive

A fragmentary file contains no listener letters. This does not establish that nobody listened. Investigate where letters were collected and whether they survived before drawing a negative reception conclusion.

Pause and reason

What can numerous shipping records demonstrate strongly?

Show the reasoning

They may document distribution organisation very well, without demonstrating persuasion or policy influence equally well.

05

Do not combine enjoyment, belief and action

Why this matters

The link between favourable interpretation and political preference is conditional.

Someone can enjoy a film while opposing the sender’s policy, or believe a report while rejecting the proposed cooperation. Cultural appreciation, source trust and policy preference are distinct judgements. Track the verbs and questions used in your sources. Rewriting “found entertaining” as “supported” enlarges the evidence by inserting a transition the original document does not establish.

Military events, trade, maritime security, domestic coalitions and diplomatic pressure can influence wartime behaviour. Cooperation following a campaign does not identify the campaign as the sole cause. Compare decision-makers’ reasons, exposure timing and other changes. Similar actors without exposure may provide a useful comparison, but substantially different contexts can make a simple comparison misleading.

Include negative and divergent cases. Intensive broadcasting without expected attitude change helps investigate a failed connection. Favourable film interpretation alongside policy opposition may reveal distinct mechanisms rather than bad data. A conclusion that communication supplied information or facilitated existing cooperation can be appropriate. Carefully supported contribution is analytically stronger than an expansive claim with no demonstrated pathway.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Two different responses

A viewer writes that a film was impressive but the government should not join the proposed agreement. The statements need not conflict: aesthetic evaluation and political choice concern different objects.

Pause and reason

Does a favourable review establish policy support?

Show the reasoning

No. A separate statement or behavioural record must address the policy preference.

06

Separate repeated circulation from independent confirmation

Why this matters

A story multiplying across channels may not add independent observations.

A radio report may be summarised in a newspaper and then enter a newsreel. Circulation can increase visibility, but three appearances do not establish three independent confirmations. Trace provenance, quotations and publication dates. Documents preserved in different archival boxes may copy the same original. Count sources separately from independent observations rather than treating file quantity as evidential strength.

Comparative research must specify country, period and social group. Urban newspaper readers and rural radio listeners encounter different access and interpretive conditions. Similar outcomes across countries do not establish identical mechanisms. Record programme intensity, earlier relationships, local controls and alternative causes. Unequal archival preservation also means unequal confidence; abundant documents are not a measure of greater influence.

Contemporary platforms and creators can select access and transform meaning, partly resembling earlier editors. Data collection, speed and individualised targeting nevertheless differ. Historical analogy helps identify an overlooked intermediary but cannot establish the same effect. State the comparable decision point, changed condition and evidence needed to test the proposed outcome. History then becomes a research instrument rather than a ready-made recipe.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Three papers, one source

Three newspapers publish one favourable story copied from an agency bulletin. This demonstrates circulation rather than three independent observations. Different local headlines may still be valuable evidence of mediation.

Pause and reason

Do different newspapers necessarily provide independent evidence?

Show the reasoning

No. Investigate common sources and copying before assuming independence.

Case workshop

Case workshop 1

Case 1: A success report’s inferential leap

A hypothetical report cites many screenings and favourable letters, then claims the entire target audience was persuaded.

  1. Separate screening records from letters. The former concern exhibition, the latter particular interpretations.
  2. Investigate repeat attendance, self-selection among writers and silent viewers. Do not calculate attitude percentages with an unknown denominator.
  3. Seek separate evidence for policy support rather than converting favourable cultural interpretation into political preference.
A defensible conclusion

The file may support distribution and some favourable responses. Universal persuasion exceeds what this evidence can establish.

Case workshop 2

Case 2: Campaign or commerce?

A hypothetical government signs a cooperation agreement during a media campaign. Significant commercial negotiations occur simultaneously.

  1. Date the communication and trade processes. Investigate whether the decision was already forming before the campaign.
  2. Compare information and interests mentioned in decision records. One favourable quotation cannot summarise every cause.
  3. Consider a comparable setting with different media exposure, while identifying security and economic differences limiting comparison.
A defensible conclusion

Both processes might contribute. If sources establish only commercial reasons, the campaign’s independent effect remains unproven.

Research practice

Build a media evidence chain in Excel

Use the shared workbook’s hypothetical measurement lab alongside the student evidence matrix. Assign numbers to the correct stage before interpreting them.

  1. Define a radio, film or press example explicitly as hypothetical and specify the target group.
  2. Record production, availability, actual encounter and interpretation as separate indicators.
  3. Add each indicator’s coverage and what it cannot establish.
  4. Write the strongest rival explanation and a bounded conclusion without replacing unknown values with zero.
Read a model response

Distribution records support potential access, cinema records a particular screening, and letters particular interpretations. The full audience’s attitudes and political behaviour remain unknown, so neither a public-support rate nor policy influence follows.

Questions worth asking

Are favourable letters useful at all?

Yes. They reveal interpretations, reasons and particular experiences. The mistake is treating them as a representative listener sample. Match your question to what the source can establish.

Does a screening prove exposure?

A screening record establishes exhibition. Evidence that people were present strengthens an exposure claim; attention, interpretation and persuasion still require separate support.

Does technology matter, then?

Certainly: it changes speed, cost, durability and access routes. But diplomatic effects require evidence of how people and institutions used these possibilities under particular conditions.

The academic argument

The film was screened: What did people think?

We will use the Good Neighbor Policy to examine how radio, cinema and the press transformed public diplomacy into a mass, visual and cross-border activity.

When a Policy Problem Became a Media Problem

In the 1930s, the principal United States problem in Latin America was not a lack of visibility. Military interventions, economic pressure and unequal relationships had left substantial distrust of Washington in many countries. Franklin D. Roosevelt’s Good Neighbour policy answered this inheritance with a stated commitment to non-intervention and reciprocal respect. Radio programmes, films and press materials could explain the new direction, but communication could not erase previous conduct by itself. Policy and narrative must therefore be separated analytically. A reduction in interventionist practice could create a condition for credibility; media activity sought to make that change visible and intelligible. The intention was rapprochement, but any effect depended on whether foreign publics perceived consistency between the message and American behaviour.

As the Second World War approached, the problem acquired a security dimension. Washington regarded Axis broadcasting, commercial ties and propaganda networks in the region as possible threats to hemispheric solidarity. American programmes were consequently intended to do more than make the country likeable. They explained the war, answered rival narratives, encouraged strategic cooperation and protected economic relationships. These purposes could coexist in one activity, but they were not identical outcomes. Listening to a broadcast demonstrates access; a newspaper’s use of supplied material demonstrates circulation; a government decision to cooperate is a political outcome. Schindler’s historical account encourages us to examine what institutions intended and which instruments they activated in sequence, rather than compressing these different levels into a single declaration of ‘success’.

The period should not be read as the sudden creation of a complete and coherent public diplomacy system. Earlier Pan-American networks, diplomatic missions, commercial connections and cultural relations were repurposed under wartime conditions, while new offices and temporary coordinating arrangements were added. One of Schindler’s central contributions is to make the long-standing interdependence of public and private actors visible. Yet the existence of an earlier network did not make the later programme inevitable. Agencies competed for resources, jurisdiction and political support; companies defended their own interests; local partners altered programmes in practice. The object of analysis is therefore not a smooth path of progress, but the contingent selection, combination and limitation of inherited capacity in response to a new problem. A more precise chronology clarifies the difference between policy and publicity. Roosevelt announced the Good Neighbour approach after entering office in 1933, while the non-intervention principle endorsed at the Seventh International Conference of American States in Montevideo that year strengthened regional expectations. The end of the United States occupation of Haiti and the termination in 1934 of the arrangement associated with the Platt Amendment offered observable support for the new language, although economic asymmetry and memories of earlier interventions remained. Students should therefore ask which policy change was visible to which public and through what channel. Presidential speeches reveal intention, agreements document official conduct, and local newspapers show how conduct was publicly framed. Placing these sources together prevents a symbolic declaration from being mistaken for completed political change and shows that communication could amplify credible behaviour but could not substitute for it.

Institutions, Markets and Local Intermediaries

The body created in 1940 and renamed the Office of the Coordinator of Inter-American Affairs in 1941 is commonly identified in the literature as the OCIAA or CIAA. Led by Nelson Rockefeller, it attempted to coordinate security, economic, health, cultural and media activities directed towards the Western Hemisphere. It was not a propaganda ministry operating alone with unlimited authority. Jurisdictions sometimes overlapped with those of the Department of State, diplomatic missions, wartime information bodies and other federal agencies. Disputes over budgets, personnel and authority could shape what reached the field. A tidy organisation chart is therefore not evidence of effective coordination. Internal correspondence, resource decisions and overseas implementation must be examined together before a researcher concludes that the system functioned as its formal design suggested.

Public-private cooperation greatly expanded the system’s capacity. Radio networks, advertising agencies, news organisations, film studios, distribution companies and commercial firms supplied specialist knowledge and existing routes to audiences. Government could use these networks rather than bear the full cost of constructing infrastructure from the beginning; companies might expect market access, reputational benefits or advantages within wartime regulation. Joint activity, however, did not mean a common purpose. A studio’s concern with box-office performance, a broadcaster’s interest in listener loyalty and a public agency’s security objective imposed different limits on the same content. Researchers should consequently ask not only whether a contract existed, but who could alter a script, who controlled distribution and who carried the financial or political risk. These questions reveal the practical allocation of power within a nominal partnership.

Latin American journalists, translators, artists, radio presenters, cinema operators, officials and audiences were also constitutive actors in the system. They were not merely the final recipients of a completed message. They judged which words sounded natural, which images might provoke resistance and which issues had local importance. Some adapted American material, some rejected it, and some reframed it for their own political or commercial purposes. Moreover, the ‘Latin American public’ was not a single audience: country, class, race, generation, urban-rural divisions and access to media affected reception. The approval of a local editor cannot represent an entire society, while a government censorship decision does not directly reveal private attitudes. Analysis must therefore treat intermediaries as sources of access and credibility, but also as actors with the power to transform meaning. Mapping the programme as a network makes institutional power easier to analyse. The body established in 1940 initially coordinated commercial and cultural relations; its renamed wartime form under Rockefeller acquired a broader remit in 1941. Instructions moved among Washington, embassies and field offices, while film studios, broadcasters, advertising specialists and local partners introduced their own professional rules. Students can label four different links on an actor map: finance, formal authority, control over content and access to audiences. The same organisation may be powerful on one link and dependent on another. A contract proves sponsorship, not control of the final meaning. Local expertise could improve linguistic and cultural fit, yet unequal resources could constrain genuine reciprocity. Public-private coordination must consequently be assessed both as added capacity and as a governance arrangement that distributed risk, credit and accountability unevenly.

The Different Operating Logics of Radio, Film and the Press

Radio could cross borders rapidly and potentially establish a regular relationship with listeners. Yet a long technical and social chain separated programme production from actual listening: transmitter power, frequency, time zone, receiver ownership, electricity, interference, language and trust in the presenter’s voice all mattered. Local stations or familiar speakers could lend credibility to a programme, but they could also change its content. If news broadcasting became little more than the repetition of favourable claims, listeners could turn to alternative sources. Radio’s mechanism was therefore not repetition alone; availability, regularity, intelligibility and journalistic credibility had to work together. Listener letters provide useful evidence of response, but their authors are self-selecting and cannot automatically be treated as representative of the full audience.

Film could place policy within compelling combinations of image, sound and narrative. Newsreels and documentaries explained current events, while fiction and entertainment conveyed more indirect images of modernity, family, consumption and social order. Production nevertheless did not guarantee exhibition in a target country. Studio negotiations, content review, dubbing or subtitling, censorship, print distribution and cinema scheduling were separate decision points. An alteration intended to remove stereotypes might introduce a new simplification, and local viewers could read an image of ‘friendship’ as artificial or self-interested. Screening totals measure exposure, not interpretation. Any claim of influence requires evidence from reviews, box-office records, audience research and local cultural production, with attention to what each source can represent. The immersive quality of film was an affordance, not proof of persuasion.

Press activity included news bulletins, photographs, articles, magazines, pamphlets and links with news agencies. Printed material could offer detailed explanation and gain legitimacy through a local editor, but literacy, circulation, paper shortages, political alignment and censorship constrained access. A supplied text might not be published; if published, it might be shortened or reframed by a critical headline. Media also worked across one another. A radio speech could be summarised in a newspaper, a film star promoted through a magazine interview, or a newsreel connected to a commercial campaign. Such cross-media repetition could increase visibility, but the appearance of the same claim in several channels was not independent confirmation. Researchers must trace the original source, local editing and the different publics reached by each channel. Concrete examples should illuminate rather than stand in for the whole system. Disney's Saludos Amigos, released in 1942, and The Three Caballeros, released in 1944, joined entertainment to themes of hemispheric friendship and emerged from this wider environment of cooperation. They do not represent every film or every audience response. Likewise, American short-wave capacity and links with local stations expanded possible radio access, while receiver ownership and signal quality still varied. A useful comparison records for each medium its production cost, distribution speed, localisation needs, feedback route and durability. Radio may be rapid but fleeting; print can be reread but depends on literacy and paper; film can create a shared spectacle but requires prints, cinemas and scheduling. These affordances generate hypotheses about possible influence, not predetermined effects, and each hypothesis requires evidence from the place where reception occurred.

Reception, Outcomes and Rival Explanations

Foreign publics did not assess messages on a blank slate. Memories of American intervention, economic inequality, racial hierarchies and stereotypical Hollywood representations of Latin America shaped the interpretation of new claims about friendship. Conduct consistent with the Good Neighbour policy might reduce some suspicions, whereas discriminatory or patronising practices could undermine the official narrative. The same campaign could evoke partnership in one audience and wartime opportunism in another. ‘A positive message was produced’ cannot therefore be converted into ‘a positive attitude resulted’. Reception should be separated into liking, believing, remembering, discussing and acting. Evidence of one does not establish the others. Collapsing these stages makes public diplomacy appear more certain and more powerful than the available evidence allows.

A robust assessment separates intention and context from the operational evidence chain: activity, output, technical availability or potential reach, verified exposure, reception and interpretation, intermediate outcome, decision or behaviour and longer-term impact. Agency files may be strong on intention, activity and output but weak on later stages; transmitter and distribution records can establish availability without proving exposure. Local newspapers and personal letters show particular responses yet present problems of representativeness. Diplomatic reporting conveys field observations while reflecting the officer’s contacts and institutional expectations. Production totals, technical records, content analysis, local sources and decision records must therefore complement one another, with every finding assigned explicitly to the stage it can support. This prevents the most abundant archive from defining the answer simply because it survived.

Media programmes were not the only plausible cause of wartime rapprochement. The military course of the war, maritime security, trading opportunities, domestic political coalitions, diplomatic pressure and inherited Pan-American relationships operated at the same time. A government’s cooperation with the United States does not prove that its population liked American films or was persuaded by a broadcast. Conversely, critical press coverage does not establish the absence of interstate cooperation. Rival explanations can be tested through chronology, decision-makers’ stated reasoning, comparisons among countries and examination of places or sectors in which a programme was absent. A defensible conclusion will often be modest: under specified conditions, the campaign supplied information, reduced uncertainty or facilitated an existing disposition to cooperate. That is analytically stronger than claiming that media alone transformed opinion. Reception should be disaggregated across countries and social groups. Mexico and Brazil pursued wartime relations with the United States through their own calculations about security, trade, industrialisation and domestic legitimacy; their entry into war against the Axis did not occur at the same moment or for one common reason. Media activity cannot simply be credited with those choices. Researchers must consider diplomatic bargaining, commodity agreements, submarine attacks and existing political coalitions. Urban newspaper readers, organised workers, rural radio listeners and young cinema-goers also had unequal access to the campaign. Contemporary polling, where available, requires scrutiny of sampling and question wording. Diaries, letters and reviews reveal interpretation in depth but not necessarily its prevalence. A strong design combines chronology, different source types and negative cases, including settings where intensive programming did not produce the expected attitude. Such cases help identify the conditions under which a proposed mechanism failed.

Synthesis: Media Power, Credibility and Evidence

The case demonstrates that the technical properties of a medium are not diplomatic effects. Radio offers rapid and repeated contact, film an intensive visual experience, and the press detail plus local editorial legitimacy. None produces trust independently. Credibility is a relationship jointly shaped by observed policy, the verifiability of a message, the reputation of the sending institution and the judgement of local intermediaries. Schindler’s focus on connections among government, private actors and foreign publics is especially valuable here. Complementary work in media history and cultural relations makes the infrastructure of distribution and the interpretive agency of audiences more visible. The resulting synthesis rejects technological determinism: wider reach increases the possibility of contact, not the certainty of persuasion or policy influence.

In the seminar exercise, students will follow one radio programme, film or press package as an ‘evidence file’. They will first identify the problem to which the material responded and the actor that commissioned it. They will then map the producer, translator, distributor and local gatekeeper. In the third stage they will seek separate evidence for the output, technical availability, verified exposure, observed reception and any plausible political consequence. Each claim will be labelled as a documented fact, a convergent finding, a reasonable inference or an unresolved proposition. Counter-evidence and archival silence will also be recorded. This procedure replaces the repetition of an agency’s success report with a mechanism-based explanation of where the process operated, where it failed and how confidently we can know.

The transferable lesson is not simply that old media have been replaced by digital tools. Platforms, search systems, content creators and online community managers now select access and transform meaning in ways partly analogous to editors and distributors, but speed, data collection and micro-targeting are markedly different. Historical comparison generates a useful question: which intermediary between sender and public has been rendered invisible? It does not justify assuming an identical outcome. When making a transfer, students should specify the analogous mechanism, the changed context and the observable evidence that the proposed effect would produce. This discipline turns the Good Neighbour experience from a ready-made formula for success into a critical instrument for evaluating contemporary strategies. Students can consolidate the week by constructing an evidence matrix. Rows represent intention, activity, output, availability, exposure, reception, intermediate outcome and behaviour; columns represent policy papers, contracts, technical logs, distribution lists, surveys, letters, local press and decision records. Every cell should state the source's date, geographical coverage and social reach, then classify the support as direct, indirect or absent. The exercise shows why abundant documentation is not synonymous with strong causal evidence: hundreds of distribution forms may document circulation exceptionally well while saying almost nothing about persuasion. The final step is to formulate the strongest rival explanation and identify an observation capable of weakening it. Consistency between policy and message may sustain credibility, whereas contradictory conduct may erase communicative gains. The Good Neighbour experience is valuable not as a universal recipe, but as a disciplined demonstration that institutions, intermediaries and audiences jointly shaped contingent results.

Research seminar

Research question

At what evidentiary threshold does the expansion of wartime radio, film, and press production permit an inference that trust was built among foreign publics?

Competing interpretations

Compare an approach that treats distribution and reach as outcome measures with one that examines local intermediaries' selection, reception, and behavioral change as separate stages.

Source criticism

Examine the Office of War Information film Tuesday in November (1944) via its NARA catalog record (local identifier 306.201) and the Archives viewing link. Record Group 229 in NARA's ‘America Abroad’ guide describes a different film collection; it is not the same record as this OWI film. The film's narrative does not directly show European viewers' response or impact.

Seminar task

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, give Tuesday in November's title, date, and archival identifier 306.201; separate direct observation from catalog or institutional inference about its producer, intended European audience, and circulation. Classify the film using Cull's distinction between broadcasting and cultural diplomacy. Against the rival claim that screening numbers prove impact, specify two independent record types needed for local distribution and audience response.

Synthesis

  1. Technical availability, verified exposure, attention, trust and persuasion are distinct stages.
  2. Local intermediaries select and reinterpret messages conveyed through radio, cinema and the press.
  3. The Good Neighbor Policy combined an anti-interventionist discourse with multi-channel cultural diplomacy.
  4. Public-private partnerships can increase capacity while also creating problems of responsibility and representation.
  5. The production, distribution and reception of material, and its political effects, must be demonstrated through separate evidence.

Questions for discussion

  1. Apart from the content of a message, which factors determine the credibility of a radio broadcast?
  2. To what extent was the Good Neighbor Policy a reciprocal relationship, and to what extent was it an attempt to exert strategic influence?
  3. Which of cinema, radio and the press gives audiences the greatest scope for reinterpretation?

Sources and critical reading

Read these alongside the explanations. A source can document an intention or an activity without establishing reception or impact. Original publication titles are retained for accurate identification.

  1. Nicholas J. Cull, Public Diplomacy: Lessons from the Past

    §2.3 cultural diplomacy, p. 19; §2.5 international broadcasting, p. 21.

    Cull distinguishes the overseas transmission of cultural resources from engagement with foreign publics through radio, television and the internet.

  2. U.S. National Archives, America Abroad: Motion Pictures

    Inspect the film and newsreel collection descriptions; an archival guide is not evidence of reception.

    The National Archives guide identifies film collections for overseas outreach; catalogue descriptions do not show that audiences watched or were influenced.

  3. Office of War Information, Tuesday in November (1944), NARA 306.201

    Watch the film linked from the National Archives page and code its selection of electoral themes; the embedded film is the primary item.

    This OWI film belongs to the series screened in Europe; it does not document OCIAA activity in Latin America or audience reception.
