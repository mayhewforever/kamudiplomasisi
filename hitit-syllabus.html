Aim

This course examines public diplomacy and soft power through historical cases and source criticism. Students distinguish power resources from communication instruments and observable outcomes, and assess actors’ intentions, intended publics, foreign reception and decision-making processes. They test theoretical claims against original texts and historical documents, identifying rival explanations and evidentiary limits.

Content

The course begins with hard, soft, smart and relational power. It examines Benjamin Franklin in France; Union and Confederate engagement with foreign publics during the American Civil War; humanitarian relief in Cuba; Pan-American educational exchanges; the Committee on Public Information and its post-war legacy; foundations and expert networks; DCR, CIAA and OWI; radio, film, the press and the Good Neighbour policy; and Smith–Mundt and institutionalisation in 1945–1948. The final weeks compare patterns drawn from US history with institutions in the United Kingdom and Germany, before addressing evaluation, ethical limits and the final case study.

Teaching methods

Each seminar combines a short conceptual introduction, source analysis using texts or images, discussion of claims and missing causal links, a mapping or audit workshop, and a concluding assessment expressed as a cautious finding. Students prepare weekly evidence briefs. Lecture notes and assigned original sources form the core work; videos, podcasts, flashcards, quizzes and games provide optional review and self-assessment.

Five learning outcomes

  1. Explain the differences among hard, soft and smart power.
  2. Distinguish public diplomacy from propaganda, diplomacy and public relations.
  3. Identify audiences, instruments and mechanisms in historical cases and compare the roles of state and non-state actors.
  4. Construct an evidence chain from activity and reception to policy outcome.
  5. Evaluate a public-diplomacy practice in terms of ethics and measurement.

14-week plan

1 · Understanding power: hard, soft and relational power

Before defining public diplomacy, we distinguish power, attraction, persuasion and context.

Preparation: Caitlin E. Schindler, ‘Reconnecting the Past and Present’, selections from pp. 1–39. Before class, bring one everyday example of ‘attraction’ and one of ‘coercion’. Place a power resource, a communication activity, and an observable foreign-audience outcome in three separate columns.

Workshop: Power cards: classify eight foreign-policy examples by resource, mechanism and possible outcome.

2 · Before public diplomacy: Benjamin Franklin and France

Reputation, networks, print circulation and engagement with French publics.

Preparation: Schindler, ‘America’s First Public Diplomat’, pp. 41–60. Choose one image and distinguish in two sentences between what it shows and what it merely implies. Separate the commissioners' 1776 request from the later timing of Saratoga and the 1778 alliance; do not treat Franklin's personal reputation as a sufficient cause.

Workshop: Evidence or inference? Classify which claims are supported by Franklin’s portrait, a letter and the treaty document.

12 · 1945–1948: Smith–Mundt and permanent public diplomacy

The transition from a wartime apparatus to statutory authority, institutionalisation and limits on target publics.

Preparation: Schindler, ‘Public Diplomacy in Chaos and Ambiguity’, pp. 266–275, and a summary of the Smith–Mundt Act. 13 · Historical patterns: institutions in the UK and Germany

Testing patterns drawn from US history against the governance of British and German cultural institutes and broadcasters.

Preparation: Schindler, ‘Foreign Public Engagement: An American Tradition in Context’, pp. 277–295. For two institutions, propose an independent record type that could test the gap between formal purpose and practice. Evidence, reciprocity and uncertainty when carrying historical lessons into the digital era.

Source use and attendance

Students should distinguish intentions, activities, reach, reception and policy outcomes. Institutional self-descriptions should not be treated as proof of success, and historical documents should be examined in relation to their dates, producers, recipients and contexts. Later videos and comparative examples should not be used as direct evidence for the historical case under examination. Institutional access may be required for assigned book chapters and articles.

The form specifies attendance at at least 70% of theoretical classes. Portal time is not official contact time. Weekly pages link to sources and the core book.
