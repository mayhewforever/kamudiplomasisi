Week 08 · Start from scratch

Why can a truthful message still be problematic?

01Main idea02Terms03Steps04Example05Practice

The main idea in a minute

This week, we move beyond sorting messages into true and false. We also ask who is speaking, what information they select, and whether listeners can object. During the First World War, the Committee on Public Information (CPI) explained war aims; it was not giving a neutral lesson. That purpose does not make every statement a lie.

Think of an everyday example

Imagine a hypothetical advertisement for a used bicycle. The seller accurately describes its new tyres but omits its broken brakes. One sentence is true; the picture offered to the buyer is incomplete. Political communication also requires separate checks of truth and adequate context.

What will you learn?

  • Explain information, open advocacy and propaganda through practical questions.
  • Evaluate accuracy, source disclosure and freedom to object separately.
  • Distinguish an institution's closure from the disappearance of communication skills.

Start with these four terms

01Advocacy
Communication that openly supports a position or policy. Supporting something does not necessarily mean lying.
Example: Hypothetical: An identified municipal advertisement supports a new transport plan.
02Propaganda
Here, systematic communication intended to direct political thinking. Its methods require a separate ethical assessment.
Example: Hypothetical: A campaign repeats one political message in posters and speeches.
03Source transparency
Being able to identify who prepared and financed a message.
Example: Hypothetical: An apparently local advertisement discloses its foreign sponsor.
04Audience autonomy
People's ability to compare information and decide without pressure.
Example: Hypothetical: Readers can challenge a bulletin without punishment for criticism.

Understand it step by step

  1. 1
    Narrow the claim

    Do not immediately label an entire campaign good or bad. Choose one bulletin. Separate its verifiable statement, the author's interpretation and missing context. Distinguish information available at publication from facts learned later.

  2. 2
    Examine room for choice

    Does the reader know the sponsor? Can they consult other sources? How could an error be corrected? Ask the same questions of actors you support and oppose. Passing one test does not remove another problem.

  3. 3
    Separate responsibility and continuity

    The CPI and the period's repressive laws were not the same institution. A specific harm requires evidence linking decisions and implementation. The CPI's postwar closure does not establish that people, methods and relationships automatically disappeared.

Let's work through an example

Worked exercise: Selected successes

A hypothetical wartime bulletin reports two successful deliveries. It omits a third delivery's failure, known at publication. The institution is clearly identified.

  1. Verify the first two deliveries. This tests the accuracy of the statements.
  2. Seek a record showing the third delivery was known at publication. Your present knowledge alone is insufficient.
  3. Check whether the bulletin presents all deliveries as successful. Sponsor disclosure does not solve missing context.

A defensible conclusion: The text contains true facts but may misrepresent the overall situation. Whether it actually misled readers requires separate evidence.

A common mistake

A campaign using truthful information must be ethical.

What to remember

Accuracy matters; hidden funding, selective context and pressure require additional assessment.

No. Distinguishing editorial choice from coercion requires evidence of instructions, threats or penalties.

Explain · examine · apply

A closer academic reading

The useful question this week is not simply whether propaganda is bad. It is when a particular communication practice is accurate, defensible and accountable. During the First World War, the Committee on Public Information (CPI) explained war aims and supported mobilisation. This supplement separates content, delivery methods, audience choices and institutional inheritance so that evaluation becomes a reasoned argument.

01

Concepts are tools, not verdicts

Why this matters

Using a label instead of an explanation hides the specific problem.

No single boundary between information, advocacy and propaganda commands universal agreement. Information also involves selecting facts. Advocacy openly supports a position. Propaganda can be defined here as systematic communication intended to direct political thinking; this working definition does not declare every statement false. Defining the term begins an inquiry rather than completing an ethical judgement.

Separate three questions: What does the actor intend, what does the text say, and how does the practice operate? Claiming a public-safety purpose does not verify evidence. Providing accurate information does not establish an absence of pressure. Intention, published content and implementation require different records; using one document for all three collapses distinct claims.

Competing interpretations create a productive tension. One understands wartime communication as necessary public explanation; another emphasises unequal resources and the power to discourage criticism. Apply common criteria instead of choosing a side beforehand. Your conclusion may defend some features and criticise others. Such differentiation represents detailed reasoning, rather than an inability to decide.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical analogy: Choosing a school

A school may advocate its programme. Concealing competitors’ results differs from supplying an open comparison. The analogy clarifies conditions of choice, but does not reproduce wartime security pressures.

Pause and reason

What does the propaganda label fail to establish?

Show the reasoning

It establishes neither falsehood, coercion nor actual effects. Each needs separate criteria and evidence.

02

Separate accuracy from adequate context

Why this matters

Individually true statements can still distort a reader’s overall conclusion.

A factual claim is a proposition that records can test; a value judgement assesses what is good, justified or important. Distinguish them first. Then identify the claim’s period, comparison group and scope. Showing one success does not establish overall success. Quietly expanding a claim beyond its original scope is a common inferential error.

Omission is not invariably deception: no short text includes everything. Ask whether missing information could materially change a reasonable reader’s decision. Establish whether it was known at publication. A later discovery cannot by itself demonstrate deliberate concealment in an earlier text. Drafts, revisions and correspondence can reveal the selection process and support stronger conclusions.

A text’s potential to mislead differs from actual deception of readers. Comparison with relevant context may establish the former. The latter requires audience evidence. A critical reader might notice the omission. Present textual features, possible authorial intentions and observed interpretations separately, rather than assuming that one necessarily produced the others.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A missing denominator

A bulletin lists three successful deliveries without reporting total deliveries. Those successes may be real. An overall success rate requires totals and failures; the missing number alone does not establish intention.

Pause and reason

Does missing information prove intentional deception?

Show the reasoning

No. Investigate contemporary knowledge, selection decisions and the evidence supporting an intention claim.

03

Autonomy means practical audience choices

Why this matters

A formal right to reply may not provide an accessible, safe route for objection.

Audience autonomy means being able to compare claims, postpone judgement and disagree. It does not mean remaining uninfluenced: learning and persuasion are ordinary relationships. Problems arise when comparison is secretly restricted or refusal is penalised. Examine surrounding power and possible sanctions, rather than assessing autonomy solely through the tone of the message.

Source disclosure supports these choices. People may assess a government statement, apparently independent editorial and paid announcement differently. Concealed funding reduces their opportunity to consider the source’s interests. Disclosure nevertheless does not solve every ethical issue: an identified source can distort, intimidate or exclude criticism. Transparency matters without being sufficient by itself.

One message may exert different pressures on different groups. A secure citizen may hear patriotic encouragement, while someone already under suspicion experiences a loyalty test. Consider legal position, employment, language and media access rather than blaming audience weakness. Public silence does not automatically indicate consent; confidence, indifference or fear could each explain it.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Open sponsor, closed discussion

An identified workplace announcement invites political participation while promising to list absentees. Sponsor disclosure does not remove the possible employment pressure affecting whether participation is voluntary.

Pause and reason

Does everyone hearing a message establish autonomy?

Show the reasoning

No. Access to alternatives and the ability to refuse without pressure require separate examination.

04

Reconstruct responsibility through records

Why this matters

Treating every contemporary institution as one actor conceals actual decision pathways.

The CPI operated within a wider wartime environment of coercion. The Espionage Act and Sedition Act were legal measures distinct from the communication committee. Separate legislative, judicial and enforcement responsibilities from CPI communication. This distinction does not exempt the committee from ethical examination; it identifies the powers, decisions and practices through which particular harm arose.

Distinguish direct instruction, foreseeable contribution and response after harm became known. Central orders can support the first; incentives and repeated complaints may illuminate the second; corrections and oversight records inform the third. A document supporting one level does not conclusively establish another. Legal liability and historical or ethical contribution are also different questions.

Local speakers, editors and volunteers could transform central messages. This makes the centre neither automatically responsible for everything nor entirely exempt. Investigate guidance, oversight and opportunities to intervene. Without a direct order, contribution remains investigable but must not become certainty by assumption. Include counter-evidence, such as instructions prohibiting harmful language, in the assessment.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A harsher speech

A volunteer turns a general participation appeal into a speech targeting local opponents. Compare the central text with the speech, then investigate whether the centre learned of the incident and responded.

Pause and reason

What does failure to find a central order show?

Show the reasoning

That direct instruction remains unproven, not that every possible contribution or oversight responsibility disappears.

05

Institutional closure does not end every relationship

Why this matters

Demonstrating continuity requires more than resemblance.

The CPI did not become a permanent information ministry after the war. Legal existence, however, differs from experience carried by people. Journalists, artists and organisers may work elsewhere; distribution methods may be reused. Considering organisational discontinuity alongside professional continuity avoids treating a closure date as the end of every relationship. Each continuity claim needs evidence.

Identify the carrier, date and pathway of inheritance. Personnel records may demonstrate a person’s move; correspondence may show deliberate transfer of a method; procedural documents may reveal changed practice. Similar posters alone do not establish descent. Organisations facing similar problems may independently develop similar solutions. Resemblance generates a historical question rather than answering it.

Inheritance includes criticism as well as skills. Suspicion of propaganda formed part of the political memory affecting subsequent arrangements. Choosing the language of education or cultural relations may signal distance from earlier experience, but new conduct needs verification. Compare funding, oversight, disclosure and opportunities for response. History then becomes contingent redesign rather than inevitable professional progress.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical analogy: A dissolved club

A club closes and former members use its meeting method in another association. People and methods may continue without the original club surviving legally. State institutions require more detailed evidence of transfer.

Pause and reason

Is finding a similar later method sufficient?

Show the reasoning

No. Identify its carrier and transfer route while considering independent development.

06

Turn ethical assessment into a reasoned decision

Why this matters

Good intentions, success and ethical acceptability belong in different columns.

Define a narrow act: concealed sponsorship, omission of a setback or discouragement of criticism. Record findings separately for accuracy, disclosure, audience choice, foreseeable harm and correction. A total score may hide a serious violation behind minor benefits. Explain which principle concerns which established feature, so readers can assess the argument rather than merely its conclusion.

Proportionality compares an objective with the burden imposed by its methods. Urgency may warrant brevity, not unlimited concealment. Ask whether a feasible, less harmful alternative could achieve the purpose. Judge feasibility within contemporary conditions. Neither giving historical actors all present knowledge nor treating common historical practice as an automatic excuse produces adequate assessment.

You may recommend continuation, revision or termination, then state the strongest objection and the evidence that could change your decision. This is accountable reasoning rather than indecision. Keep effectiveness separate: a method can work while remaining ethically problematic, or meet ethical requirements without producing persuasion. These judgements call for different questions and different evidence.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A delayed correction

An institution corrects an error through a small notice. Correction is a positive finding, but assess whether original recipients could encounter it and whether delay caused further harm.

Pause and reason

Must an ethical practice be effective?

Show the reasoning

No. Defensibility of the method and observed outcomes are separate assessments.

Case workshop

Case workshop 1

Case 1: Undisclosed funding

In this hypothetical case, a local newspaper publishes accurate war news with outside financial support, without disclosing the supporter.

  1. Verify factual content and funding separately. Accurate reporting does not establish adequate audience knowledge of the source.
  2. Investigate whether the funder intervened in selection. Financial support does not automatically mean every sentence was directed.
  3. Consider disclosure as an alternative. Explain which audience judgement sponsorship might affect without assuming the actual reaction.
A defensible conclusion

A limited conclusion identifies a transparency problem. Falsehood, complete control and mass persuasion do not follow from the scenario.

Case workshop 2

Case 2: Closed institution, surviving method

A hypothetical researcher finds a former employee in a later information office. The two institutions used similar posters.

  1. Establish the personnel link through records. One person’s move does not demonstrate survival of the whole structure.
  2. Seek training notes, correspondence or explicit references showing methodological transfer. Visual resemblance admits independent explanations.
  3. Compare the later office’s purposes, authority and oversight. The same technique can operate differently under different rules.
A defensible conclusion

Personnel continuity may be demonstrated while organisational and purposive continuity remain unresolved. Defining the transfer narrowly strengthens the historical explanation.

Research practice

Build a reasoned ethical dossier in Excel

Use the shared workbook’s student evidence matrix to assess a hypothetical message. Separate judgements before considering scores.

  1. Enter the message’s date, source and precise claim; identify the scenario as hypothetical.
  2. Record available and missing evidence concerning accuracy, disclosure and opportunities for objection.
  3. Write the strongest counterargument and a feasible alternative causing less harm.
  4. Record a provisional judgement, its revision conditions and what remains unknown about effectiveness.
Read a model response

The message contains verifiable facts, but undisclosed funding prevents adequate source assessment. Sponsorship should be disclosed and correction procedures made visible. No evidence establishes persuasion, so an effects claim is unwarranted.

Questions worth asking

Is ethical evaluation just personal opinion?

Values enter judgement. Stating the principle, verified facts and reasons for rejecting an alternative makes the evaluation contestable and accountable rather than merely personal.

Does missing evidence establish innocence?

Historical inquiry can conclude that a claim has not been demonstrated. Without investigating why records are absent, neither definite guilt nor complete absence of contribution follows.

Does war justify all secrecy?

No. Investigate a specific security reason, duration, scope and less restrictive options. Withholding an operational detail differs from knowingly providing a false account.

The academic argument

Why can a truthful message still be problematic?

This week discusses the ethical boundaries of public diplomacy by examining the CPI experience in terms of truthfulness, freedom, target-audience autonomy and institutional legacy.

The Boundary between Information and Propaganda

There is no universally accepted line separating information from propaganda. A message may contain individually accurate facts while directing an audience towards a misleading conclusion through selection, omission, repetition or emotional framing. Conversely, an openly persuasive argument need not be unethical simply because it advocates a policy. The CPI’s leadership emphasised truthfulness, partly because credibility was a practical resource in wartime. Yet the committee did not offer neutral instruction: it sought to explain American aims, sustain mobilisation and counter opposing narratives. Ethical analysis must therefore ask not only whether statements were technically true, but whether audiences received sufficient context to judge them independently.

Source transparency is a second boundary. People assess evidence partly by knowing who produced, financed and distributed it. Governments often work through newspapers, experts, voluntary associations or cultural figures because these intermediaries possess access and trust. Such cooperation may be legitimate, but credibility borrowed from an intermediary becomes deceptive when official sponsorship is deliberately concealed. Disclosure does not solve every problem; an identified government source can still distort evidence. It does, however, allow audiences to recognise interests and make a more informed judgement. Schindler’s attention to mixed public–private networks makes the question of attribution central rather than incidental. A practical reading protocol is to underline each verifiable claim, circle every value judgment and list the context needed to assess omission. The distinctions make ethical disagreement discussable rather than intuitive.

Purpose, content and method should consequently be examined separately. A legitimate purpose cannot excuse fabricated evidence, while accurate content can be delivered through intimidation or exclusion. Emotional communication is not automatically improper: war, loss and danger are genuinely emotional subjects. The ethical risk arises when fear is exaggerated to close debate, social groups are stigmatised or uncertainty is hidden in order to manufacture confidence. The label propaganda can itself be used strategically to discredit an opponent while exempting one’s own persuasion from scrutiny. The most useful question is not whether an item deserves one fixed label. It is which features increase or diminish truthfulness, openness and audience autonomy, and whether less harmful methods could accomplish the stated public purpose. For close analysis, students should separate factual accuracy from completeness, framing and procedural fairness. A sentence may be literally correct while omitting the scale of losses, the existence of uncertainty or the strongest counterargument. Framing determines which causes and consequences appear relevant; repetition and emotional imagery can make one interpretation feel inevitable even when alternatives remain available. Source disclosure matters because audiences evaluate a government bulletin, an independent editorial and a paid placement differently. Ethical communication therefore requires a correction rule: who acknowledges an error, how quickly, through the same channels or not, and with what prominence? This procedural question turns credibility from a vague reputation into an institutional practice. Analysts should also avoid deciding ethics from the speaker's benevolent intention. They must reconstruct what information a reasonable recipient possessed, what choices the message encouraged, and whether withheld context would have materially changed those choices. That method permits criticism without treating every attempt at persuasion as inherently illegitimate.

Wartime Mobilisation and Civil Liberties

The CPI operated within a coercive wartime environment, but institutional relationships must be described accurately. The Espionage Act of 1917 and Sedition Act of 1918 were legal measures distinct from the committee. Courts, prosecutors, postal authorities, employers and local organisations exercised powers that the CPI did not itself possess. It would therefore be wrong to attribute every restriction on dissent directly to Creel’s organisation. It would be equally misleading to isolate official communication from the surrounding climate. Government rhetoric, patriotic campaigns, press practices and law could reinforce one another, making opposition appear not simply mistaken but disloyal.

Anti-war activists, socialists and some immigrant communities faced surveillance, suspicion or social sanction. Foreign-language communication illustrates the double edge of inclusion. Translation could provide access to public information, recognise linguistic diversity and invite participation. It could also mark particular communities as populations requiring supervision or proof of loyalty. The ethical question is thus not solved by the existence of outreach. Researchers must examine how groups were represented, whether they could answer, what consequences followed disagreement and whether programme design respected them as citizens and interpreters rather than as risks to be managed.

Responsibility in a network is distributed but not dissolved. A local speaker, newspaper editor or employer might intensify a central message in ways the CPI did not expressly order. The centre is not automatically responsible for every act; nor can it disclaim foreseeable harms produced by the system it mobilised. Analysis should consider guidance, incentives, oversight, complaints and corrective action. Did leaders warn against stigmatisation? Could an affected group seek redress? Were partners removed after abuse? These questions connect institutional design to civil liberties and prevent both total blame and total exoneration from replacing evidence about causal pathways. The wartime setting also requires a precise map of power. The CPI produced and coordinated persuasive communication, whereas Congress enacted the Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918, courts interpreted those laws, and postal and law-enforcement authorities applied coercive powers. Keeping those institutions distinct prevents a false claim that every restriction was ordered by Creel's committee. It does not, however, remove the CPI from the surrounding ecology of pressure. Official accusations could stigmatise dissenters; volunteer speakers could expose neighbours who remained silent; and highly charged publicity could make private harassment more likely. Students should therefore test three levels of responsibility: direct command, foreseeable contribution and response after harm became visible. They should also examine unequal vulnerability. A message heard as patriotic encouragement by a socially secure citizen might be experienced as a threat by an immigrant, labour organiser or critic already subject to surveillance. Ethical evaluation must include those differentiated audience conditions rather than infer consent from public quiet.

The CPI’s Institutional and Political Legacy

The CPI was dismantled rapidly after the Armistice and did not become a permanent ministry of information. Formal discontinuity, however, did not mean that its experience disappeared. Officials, writers, artists, advertisers, film-makers and voluntary organisers carried skills and professional relationships into other institutions. Techniques of audience segmentation, visual persuasion, news coordination and work through intermediaries remained available. Later agencies could learn from this repertoire without descending from the CPI through an unbroken bureaucratic line. Schindler’s account therefore encourages a distinction between continuity of organisation and continuity of people, practices and assumptions.

The committee also left a negative inheritance. The intensity of wartime persuasion contributed to public suspicion of government efforts to shape opinion in peacetime. Later programmes often described themselves as cultural relations, education or objective information partly to mark distance from propaganda. These names expressed aspirations, but they cannot by themselves prove a change in conduct. Historians must compare funding, personnel, source disclosure, target audiences, opportunities for response and oversight. A renamed activity may retain earlier methods, while an institution that borrows a technique may place it within substantially different rules and purposes.

Post-war retrenchment returned much foreign engagement to universities, foundations, news organisations and professional associations. That shift did not restore a simple private sphere untouched by government. Diplomatic officials still facilitated contacts; public and private priorities sometimes converged; and international crises created pressure for renewed coordination. Relationships could survive institutional closure because they were held by people and organisations rather than by an official chart alone. The legacy was therefore non-linear: wartime centralisation, institutional dissolution, dispersed continuity and later recombination. Understanding this pattern prevents the story from becoming a march towards an inevitable permanent agency. It also reveals how democratic anxiety about propaganda helped shape the language and structures through which later American institutions sought credibility. Institutional legacy is best traced through several kinds of continuity rather than a single family tree. Personnel may move to later agencies; techniques such as film distribution, speaker bureaux and press coordination may be reused; archives and practical lessons may remain available; and political memory may influence later debates about government information. These continuities can travel separately. A later organisation can adopt a technique while rejecting the CPI's temporary mandate, or employ former staff under stronger oversight. Discontinuity matters equally: post-war demobilisation, public suspicion of propaganda and changed law can interrupt organisational inheritance. A careful legacy claim therefore identifies the carrier, the date and the mechanism of transfer. Similar-looking posters are not sufficient evidence of descent. Students should look for correspondence, personnel records, budget lines, procedural manuals and explicit references by later officials. This approach replaces the comforting story of steady professional progress with a contingent history in which capacity, criticism and institutional redesign develop together.

A Framework for Ethical Assessment

A practical ethical framework begins with accuracy and context. Are central claims verifiable, are uncertainties acknowledged and are relevant facts omitted in ways that change the overall meaning? The second criterion is transparency: can audiences identify the producer, funder and important intermediaries? A third concerns plural access. People need realistic opportunities to encounter competing evidence and criticism, especially when the state possesses exceptional resources. None of these criteria requires officials to be neutral about policy. They require persuasion to remain answerable to evidence and to recognise the audience’s capacity for judgement.

A fourth criterion is proportionality. Urgency may justify speed, concise messages or temporary limits on operational detail, but it does not make every technique necessary. Analysts should compare the expected public benefit with harms such as panic, stigma or suppression, and ask whether a less intrusive method was available. The fifth criterion is accountability over time. An ethical institution needs procedures to correct false claims, publish revisions, receive complaints and assess unexpected harm while a campaign is running. A correction that appears only after political advantage has been gained may protect the historical record without restoring the audience’s earlier choices.

Audience autonomy unites these tests. Autonomy does not mean that communication has no persuasive purpose; much democratic speech attempts to persuade. It means that people are treated as agents capable of comparing claims, withholding agreement and voicing criticism without punishment. The concept also demands attention to power. A message from a foreign government, employer or wartime authority carries pressures different from those of an ordinary speaker. Ethical judgement should therefore examine the real options surrounding reception, not simply the words on the page. Transparency, alternatives and redress turn respect for the audience from an aspiration into observable institutional practice. A usable ethical framework should operate before, during and after a campaign. Before action, officials must state a legitimate public purpose, test whether communication is necessary and consider a less intrusive alternative. During action, they should verify claims, identify sponsorship, preserve meaningful audience choice, limit emotional pressure and monitor harms across different groups. Afterwards, they must correct errors, disclose relevant records, investigate complaints and decide whether emergency authority should expire. Proportionality links all three stages: a serious and imminent danger may justify urgency, but it does not license unlimited deception or permanent restriction. The framework also distinguishes secrecy from falsehood. Temporary non-disclosure of operational details can sometimes be defended, while knowingly supplying a false account creates a different and usually heavier burden. Students should record who made each judgment and what evidence was available at the time; otherwise later ethical review becomes an exercise in attributing success to good motives and failure to unforeseeable circumstances.

Ethics Jury: Applying the Criteria

In the classroom ethics jury, groups assess three practices: an accurate pamphlet that omits military setbacks, a message distributed through an intermediary without revealing government funding and a local speech that uses fear to discourage dissent. Each jury identifies the public purpose, evaluates truthfulness and context, tests source transparency, considers access to alternatives and estimates foreseeable harm. It must choose among continuing, revising or stopping the practice and then state the strongest objection to its own verdict. Requiring a counterargument prevents ethical analysis from becoming a quick expression of approval or dislike.

The evidence supplied to the jury should distinguish intention, implementation and outcome. An agency’s memorandum may show that leaders sought unity, but not how a volunteer delivered the message. A local report may document stigmatisation, but further evidence may be needed to connect that harm to central guidance. Students should allocate responsibility among the producer, funder, intermediary and authority responsible for oversight. They should also ask what each actor could reasonably foresee and correct. This method recognises shared responsibility without assuming that every participant in a network possessed equal knowledge or power.

The final comparison places ethical and effectiveness judgements side by side. Concealing sponsorship may increase short-term acceptance yet damage trust when disclosure occurs. Permitting criticism may complicate mobilisation while improving accuracy and long-term legitimacy. These are empirical possibilities rather than excuses: claims about necessity or benefit still require evidence. Students should therefore record who bears the cost of each option, whether that burden is reversible and which voices remain absent from the evidence. The CPI case ultimately shows why credibility is both an ethical relationship and a strategic asset. An institution that regards publics only as targets may achieve rapid circulation but undermine the durable trust on which later engagement depends. Ethical constraints are therefore not external decorations; they help constitute the kind of relationship public diplomacy claims to build. In an ethics jury, a verdict should be a reasoned finding rather than a vote on whether a historical actor seems admirable. Teams first formulate the narrow act under review—for example, an undisclosed government placement, an exaggerated atrocity claim or a volunteer speech that pressures dissenters. They then build a dossier containing the original item, production instructions, legal context, distribution route and evidence of reception. Each criterion receives a separate finding supported by a citation, and uncertainty is marked rather than silently converted into innocence or guilt. The jury must consider a feasible alternative: could the legitimate objective have been achieved through attributed, accurate and less coercive communication? Finally, it recommends a remedy such as correction, disclosure, independent review, compensation or termination of the practice. Requiring a remedy connects historical diagnosis to institutional design. It also demonstrates that ethical judgment can be rigorous without pretending that today's observers possess every fact available to wartime decision-makers.

Research seminar

Research question

Does factual accuracy suffice to answer the critique of propaganda in the CPI's communication with the foreign press, and how can credibility and audience autonomy be tested?

Competing interpretations

Compare a normative approach focused on the factual accuracy of the message with one that also considers selection, framing, authority over distribution, and the recipient's agency; do not use the label ‘propaganda’ as a judgment in advance.

Source criticism

In the Division of Foreign Press report in FRUS Public Diplomacy, document 12, examine the institution's own account of selection and distribution processes. This internal report is not direct evidence of what foreign newspapers published or what their readers thought.

Seminar task

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, assess two selected operations from the report separately against accuracy, attribution, target audience, and scope for correction, using Cull's conceptual distinctions as a method. Specify the observation on which an ethical critique rests. Formulate legitimate wartime information as a counter-hypothesis and discuss what an actual issue of a foreign newspaper could change in the assessment.

Synthesis

  1. The use of accurate information does not by itself make a campaign ethical or impartial.
  2. Content, method, purpose, transparency and target-audience autonomy must be assessed against separate criteria.
  3. The CPI and the repressive wartime legal order were related, but they were not the same thing.
  4. The CPI’s legacy operated less through direct institutional continuity than through techniques, experience and political suspicion.
  5. Ethical public diplomacy requires mechanisms for correction, oversight and accountability.

Questions for discussion

  1. Can a state’s wartime dissemination of accurate information through a concealed source be considered ethical?
  2. To what extent should the CPI be held responsible for social pressures that it did not intend but could have foreseen?
  3. Should the distinction between information and propaganda rest on an institution’s intention, its methods or its effects?
