The CPI: distribution and reception of wartime communication

Analytical overview

Upon entering the First World War, the United States sought to explain its war aims and mobilise support. President Wilson established the CPI on 13 April 1917. This temporary committee combined existing communication instruments. Message volume was significant as institutional activity, but did not independently establish readership or acceptance.

Illustrative analogy

In a hypothetical analogy, school management sends an announcement to a class representative, who shortens and shares it. One student reads it, another does not encounter it, and another objects. Dispatch alone does not establish any of these distinct reception outcomes.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the CPI’s purpose and temporary status.
  • Distinguish analytically between dispatch and reader response.
  • Examine the processes through which intermediaries transform messages.

Key concepts and distinctions

01CPI
The Committee on Public Information.
Example: Led by George Creel, this organisation coordinated wartime information and persuasion work.
02Distribution
The process of transmitting prepared content to other individuals or institutions.
Example: Transmitting a statement to a foreign news agency does not prove it was read.
03Intermediary
An actor that selects, transmits or transforms a message between sender and reader.
Example: An editor may shorten a statement or add a critical headline.
04Reception
The processes through which people understand and interpret content they encounter.
Example: One reader may trust a statement while another doubts it.

Stages of analysis

  1. 1
    Situating the institution historically

    The CPI was a temporary wartime committee, distinct from a permanent foreign ministry, and addressed both domestic and foreign audiences. Describing it as the “first public diplomacy agency” requires explicit comparative criteria: does the proposed innovation concern organisational form or the beginning of communication abroad?

  2. 2
    Stages of message transmission and transformation

    An institutional report can provide evidence of production and dispatch; a foreign newspaper copy establishes publication. Comparing the two sources identifies changes in headlines, translation or length. An intermediary should not be assumed to function as a neutral channel transmitting unchanged text.

  3. 3
    Examining reader reception

    A published text cannot represent the opinions of an entire country. Reader letters and diaries can provide evidence of interpretation within their limited scope. Where attitudinal change is observed, contemporary battlefield news and other political developments require investigation as competing explanations.

Applied case analysis

Editorial headline changes and message presentation

In a hypothetical example, a CPI-like institution dispatches “Our Shared Aims”. A foreign newspaper publishes the text under “Controversial American Claims”.

  1. The dispatch record establishes the content transmitted by the institution; successful persuasion cannot be inferred from this source.
  2. The newspaper confirms publication; the altered headline demonstrates the editor’s critical presentation.
  3. Reader response remains unknown. The editor’s interpretation must not be treated as representative of all readers; additional sources are required.

Reprinting establishes republication, without independently demonstrating endorsement of the content.

Common interpretative error

“Communication with foreign publics began when the CPI was established.”

Analytical clarification

Diplomacy, newspapers and private networks examined in earlier weeks demonstrate prior communication abroad. The CPI’s novelty should be assessed in relation to the organisation of wartime communication.

Conceptual assessment

Does an institutional distribution report prove foreign readers were persuaded?

Reasoned response

A distribution report may establish dispatch; foreign readers’ interpretations and possible persuasion require different sources.

01

Wartime context and a temporary institution’s purpose

Analytical significance

Institutional design requires analysis in relation to the historical problem it addressed.

American entry into the First World War increased the administration’s need to mobilise domestic support and explain war aims abroad. Established by President Wilson and led by George Creel, the Committee on Public Information, or CPI, combined information, persuasion and mobilisation. These related functions did not involve identical activities or success measures.

Providing information supplies content about a situation; persuasion seeks to change judgements or preferences; mobilisation encourages particular actions. One speech can contain all three. Analysis must first identify the response sought by the institution. Inferring actual behaviour from declared objectives confuses design with implementation and intention with outcome, concealing the question that evaluation should investigate.

The CPI was a temporary federal wartime committee, not a permanent foreign ministry. Such a structure could combine resources rapidly while raising questions about continuity, oversight and responsibility. Institutional capacity means being able to accomplish substantial work; institutionalisation concerns authority, resources and practices becoming established over time. Extensive production may demonstrate the former without proving the latter.

Illustrative application

An emergency-coordination analogy

A hypothetical school establishes a temporary crisis communication team. Rapid publication of numerous announcements does not establish that the team is a permanent, independent institution. The analogy illustrates organisational form without encompassing the full scope of state wartime powers and public oversight.

Analytical question

Does high content production establish permanent agency status?

Reasoned response

No. Production capacity differs from legal continuity, budget arrangements and accountability structures.

02

Message circulation and intermediary decisions

Analytical significance

Analysis must explain how centrally produced content acquires divergent meanings.

The CPI used bulletins, pamphlets, posters, films and speeches. An instrument is the form carrying a message; a mechanism explains how that form might change attention, understanding or judgement. Posters can attract attention visually, while speakers add local emphasis. Listing instruments does not substitute for explaining how they might exert influence.

A text might travel through agencies, diplomatic missions and newspapers to readers along different routes. Selection, translation, shortening and refusal are possible at each stage. An editor may change text for space or give it a politically distinctive headline. Establishing which content an audience encountered requires comparison of the original text with its local publication.

Central coordination does not make local implementation identical. Common themes may acquire different interpretations; intermediary autonomy can improve access while reducing message consistency. Field responses, alterations and feedback require investigation alongside central instructions. A network’s existence alone does not measure how closely its centre controlled implementation or the meanings produced in local settings.

Illustrative application

Editorial intervention and headline modification

A hypothetical foreign newspaper publishes a statement under a critical headline. Distribution and publication have occurred, but the sender’s intended meaning may have changed. The headline and surrounding commentary constitute a reception context as significant as the text itself.

Analytical question

Must non-publication indicate political rejection?

Reasoned response

No. Technical interruption, limited space, news priorities or censorship may explain it. The specific cause requires investigation through appropriate records.

03

Testing the “first public diplomacy agency” claim

Analytical significance

Institutional classifications require comparison against explicit criteria.

“First” becomes researchable only after defining the category. First foreign-public interaction, first central coordination, first specialised organisation and first permanent agency are different claims. Earlier weeks’ diplomacy, press, relief and educational networks demonstrate interaction before the CPI. A claim that communication with foreign publics was invented in 1917 is therefore unsupported.

The CPI’s distinctive feature was organising many intermediaries and communication forms at federal level around a broad wartime purpose. Recognising its historical significance does not require endorsing every “first” claim. Mission, specialist personnel, coordination, geographical scope, continuity and legal status require separate comparison. Innovation on one criterion does not automatically confer the same distinction on all others.

Applying today’s public diplomacy concept historically can support useful comparison. Assuming contemporary actors shared the later institutional understanding risks anachronism: falsely identifying the past with subsequent concepts. Analysis must first establish the institution’s contemporary purposes and language, then specify the similarity illuminated by the later term. The concept should advance investigation while preserving historical differences.

Illustrative application

Two different meanings of “first”

In a hypothetical example, two students evaluate the same institution. One measures the initial centralisation of communication, while the other measures the establishment of permanent legal organisation. Their conclusions need not conflict; the categories and comparative criteria must first be specified.

Analytical question

Is institutional novelty equivalent to demonstrated persuasion?

Reasoned response

No. An organisation can be innovative while its effects on audience thinking remain a separate evidential question.

04

Applying source criticism to activity reports

Analytical significance

Institutional self-assessment must not be equated with an independent measure of success.

An activity report reveals both operations and how an institution wishes to present them. Information about bulletins, channels and connections is valuable. The report may nevertheless highlight outputs to justify budgets, personnel or continued authority. This possibility does not invalidate the report in its entirety; it requires deciding at which evidential level particular claims can be used.

Verification of measurement units is a fundamental component of source criticism. Copies printed, copies dispatched, distinct publications and total reprints measure different things. Sending one text through several channels does not establish unique readers. Aggregation requires prior clarification of the reporting period, geographical coverage, duplicate counts and the distinction between planned activities and completed operations.

Independent verification means more than finding an institutional report repeated elsewhere. A local newspaper demonstrates publication, an agency log receipt, and a reader letter one response. Each tests a different stage. Copied information limits independence. Missing archival records are not automatic proof of non-occurrence either; preservation conditions and the surviving collection’s coverage require investigation.

Illustrative application

Distinguishing dispatch counts from unique reach

A hypothetical report records one bulletin dispatched through both diplomatic and agency channels. Two dispatches are documented, but two distinct readerships are not established. Presenting the dispatch total as unique reach changes the unit measured by the evidence.

Analytical question

For what questions is an institutional report especially useful?

Reasoned response

Its mission understanding, instruments and recorded operations. Independent audience acceptance and political influence require additional sources.

05

Reading, interpretation and wartime consent

Analytical significance

Visible approval is insufficient evidence of free choice or causal influence.

Reception means interpreting content through existing knowledge, values and experience. Seeing a text, understanding it, trusting it and acting on it are separate stages. Foreign audiences are diverse: language, political position, war experience and local conflict can alter meaning. Publication in one newspaper establishes neither nationwide exposure nor nationwide acceptance.

Wartime conditions complicate evaluation. Censorship, social pressure, nationalism, battlefield developments and economic circumstances can operate alongside communication. Public approval may express sincere conviction, fear or expected conformity. This broader setting requires examination as a condition of reception; wartime pressures must not all be attributed to operations conducted by the CPI itself.

A causal-effect claim attributes change to the message. Timing, exposure and competing explanations therefore matter. Convergence among different sources can strengthen confidence without proving a sole cause. Sometimes the strongest conclusion is that the institution made an argument visible or contributed to a frame of debate, rather than determining the audience’s final behaviour.

Illustrative application

Uncertainty in interpreting audience silence

In a hypothetical speech, the observed behaviour is an absence of audience objection. Persuasion, indifference, apprehension or ceremonial conventions could produce the same silence. Without private diaries, subsequent letters or other behavioural evidence, no single interpretation can be treated as certain.

Analytical question

Why is silence not equivalent to consent?

Reasoned response

The same visible behaviour can arise from different motivations. Assessment of interpretation or consent requires evidence about context and independent expression.

06

Learning, correction and limits on temporary power

Analytical significance

Institutional responses to error form part of the evaluation of success.

Feedback involves more than incoming information; what an organisation does with it matters. If local criticism reaches the centre, receives evaluation and changes later text, a learning connection becomes traceable. Even an extensive network can repeat central expectations if it gathers only positive reports. Safe opportunities to communicate criticism affect information quality.

A correction mechanism specifies how errors are recorded, who decides and how corrections reach the original audience. Speed, accuracy and local adaptation may conflict; resolving these tensions cannot be reduced to a single general principle. Source transparency, authority limits, independent review and opportunities to object are separate criteria. Viewing an institutional objective as legitimate does not automatically legitimate every method.

Evaluating a temporary wartime organisation also means asking when and how emergency authority ends. High productive capacity is not sufficient justification for permanent power. A historical conclusion should combine organisational novelty, documented activity and uncertainty about reception. This approach avoids both prior acceptance of all influence claims and dismissal of substantive capacity solely because the evidence has limits.

Illustrative application

Recorded feedback and implemented correction

A hypothetical overseas representative reports a translation error. The presence of the report and correction in the subsequent publication are distinct findings. Where correction occurred, its reach among the original readers also requires investigation; amendment of the central file alone is insufficient.

Analytical question

Does collecting criticism complete the process of listening?

Reasoned response

No. Evaluation, decisions, responses and implementation leave additional traces. Feedback counts cannot substitute for these connections.

Applied case studies

Applied case studies 1

Worked case: treating reprinting as persuasion

A hypothetical file contains a statement’s dispatch record and its publication under a critical headline in a foreign newspaper. The institutional report classifies publication as success in securing foreign support.

  1. Verify production, transmission and publication separately. Each record provides information about another link in the chain.
  2. Examine the headline and surrounding commentary. Editorial criticism shows why publication cannot automatically count as endorsement.
  3. Identify absent sources on reader response and political preference. The institution’s success label does not fill that gap.
Analytical conclusion

Circulation and critical presentation can be established. The file alone does not support reader persuasion or changed foreign policy.

Applied case studies 2

Worked case: distribution capacity and limited institutional learning

In a hypothetical communication network, local representatives report a translation error, but headquarters redistributes the same text. A subsequent report evaluates only the increase in total dispatches favourably.

  1. Measure dispatch volume separately from processing feedback. More activity does not establish improved institutional learning.
  2. Investigate who received the warning and who could authorise a response. The failure may concern decision-making rather than missing information.
  3. Identify responsibility for correction and a record that would verify it. Later local publication can show actual change more clearly than central intention.
Analytical conclusion

Assess distribution and listening capacities together. Success in one does not automatically remedy weakness in the other.

Research application

Evaluating institutional claims in Excel

Use the student evidence matrix to disaggregate a hypothetical CPI-like institution’s claim that it persuaded foreign audiences. Identify sources for actual documents, and retain the distinction between exercise material and historical findings.

  1. List the required stages: production, transmission, publication, interpretation and possible behavioural change.
  2. Assign an appropriate source type to each stage, explicitly identifying evidence still missing.
  3. Add a rival explanation, such as battlefield news, local political preferences or social pressure.
  4. Narrow the concluding sentence to available evidence, specifying what new finding would strengthen or change it.
Model response

Illustrative assessment: Preparation of the text and its publication in a particular newspaper are documented. The critical local headline precludes treating publication as endorsement. Reader interpretations and behavioural change are insufficiently documented. The evidence therefore supports conclusions about communication capacity and circulation, while general persuasion or decisive political influence remains unresolved.

Conceptual and methodological questions

Can reports from a propaganda institution be used?

Yes. They are valuable for production, mission understanding and institutional self-presentation. Identify their purpose and seek other evidence for audience response.

Do evidential limits mean the institution was ineffective?

No. Unproven influence differs from ineffectiveness. Limit conclusions to documented stages and identify unresolved connections.

How can the modern concept of public diplomacy be applied to historical analysis?

The concept can be used with explicit comparative criteria. Differences between contemporary language and institutional structures and the later analytical concept must be preserved.

Academic argument

The CPI: distribution and reception of wartime communication

This week examines the Committee on Public Information, established during the First World War, in light of the claim that it was the first central organisation of modern American public diplomacy.

War, Mobilisation and the Establishment of the CPI

When the United States entered the First World War in April 1917, the Wilson administration confronted linked domestic and international communication problems. Conscription, taxation, food conservation and the deployment of troops required public cooperation at home, while foreign audiences needed an explanation of why a state long associated with neutrality had entered the conflict. President Woodrow Wilson created the Committee on Public Information and appointed journalist George Creel as its chairman. The committee connected information, persuasion and mobilisation, presenting the war as a contest over political principles as well as military power. Its work shows how communication becomes part of governing during an emergency rather than an activity added after policy has been made. Wilson established the committee by executive order on 13 April, with the secretaries of State, War and Navy as members and Creel directing its work. That composition joined civilian publicity to departments controlling diplomacy and military information. It also created an immediate question of independence: an organisation asked to explain policy relied on the same administration whose decisions it presented.

The CPI’s institutional form was unusual. It was a temporary federal committee supported by presidential authority, not a cabinet department established to conduct permanent cultural relations. Its overseas operations began with the Wireless and Cable Service in September 1917; a Foreign Section was formed at Wilson’s behest in October, and activity expanded in November and December as representatives were sent abroad. This distinction matters because operational reach and legal durability are different forms of capacity. Creel could coordinate many activities rapidly, yet the organisation lacked a settled peacetime mandate and disappeared soon after the war. Schindler therefore asks whether an agency is a formal bureaucracy, a coordinating centre or a network assembled for a crisis. The answer depends upon stated criteria, not simply the volume of material produced. Temporary status can reduce start-up delay because leaders borrow personnel, facilities and authority from existing bodies. The same arrangement complicates record keeping, legislative scrutiny and responsibility for mistakes. The CPI’s contraction after the Armistice and termination in 1919 demonstrate that impressive wartime reach did not become an uninterrupted peacetime institution, although personnel, techniques and debates survived in dispersed form.

Domestic and foreign communication also overlapped. Speeches delivered in American towns could be reported abroad, while foreign-language publications intended for immigrant communities crossed any neat division between audiences at home and overseas. Diplomatic missions, news organisations and private correspondents carried material beyond the setting for which it had first been prepared. This porous environment made coordination attractive but control elusive. A message designed to reassure one audience might alarm another, and a claim made for immediate mobilisation could shape American credibility after the war. The CPI therefore provides an early case of the enduring public diplomacy problem that communication travels farther and lasts longer than its producer intends. Researchers should therefore reconstruct actual circulation rather than infer audience from an office label. Reprints, translations, cable routes and correspondence can reveal unexpected recipients and altered meanings. Audience spillover also has a legal and ethical dimension: officials may describe work as foreign-facing while its material affects domestic debate. Clear disclosure and oversight must follow circulation in practice, not merely the geography imagined in a planning memorandum.

Institutional Network and Communication Tools

The committee assembled a wide repertoire: official bulletins, press releases, pamphlets, posters, photographs, exhibitions, films and prepared speeches. Its Four Minute Men delivered brief talks in cinemas, while the Foreign Section established overseas offices that distributed news and film, arranged speakers and supported reading rooms. This work relied on more than diplomatic posts. American businesses, the American Red Cross and the Young Men’s Christian Association, or YMCA, supplied established relationships, facilities, personnel or distribution channels that public officials could not reproduce quickly. Schindler’s emphasis is therefore institutional as well as rhetorical: the CPI’s foreign reach emerged from coordinating state authority with commercial and voluntary networks whose independent missions and reputations shaped access. Different media performed different tasks. A poster condensed a claim into a memorable image, a bulletin supplied material to editors, film joined narrative to spectacle, and a live speaker could adjust to a local room. A multichannel campaign could repeat a frame across settings, yet repetition also made errors travel widely and did not ensure that audiences interpreted each medium consistently.

Schindler’s network perspective prevents this system from being represented as a straight line from Washington to a passive public. Editors decided whether and where to publish; translators selected locally meaningful terms; exhibitors controlled access to screens; and volunteer speakers adjusted tone and emphasis. These intermediaries possessed their own reputations and interests. Their proximity could make an appeal more credible than an impersonal government notice, but it could also convert persuasion into social pressure. Central production therefore created only a starting point. Distribution, adaptation and reception occurred in settings where the CPI could guide activity without determining every meaning or response. To study intermediary agency, historians can compare a central script with a delivered speech, an English release with its translation, or a supplied article with the edited newspaper version. Differences are not simply corruption of an original message; they show how access depended on local judgement. They also locate responsibility when adaptation introduces exaggeration, exclusion or a culturally resonant argument.

The mechanism can be reconstructed in stages: central actors frame an objective, media specialists produce content, institutions and volunteers circulate it, intermediaries adapt it, audiences encounter it and some members respond. At each stage, researchers need different sources. Committee files reveal intentions and production; distribution records show where material was sent; local newspapers and diaries indicate possible exposure and interpretation; and behavioural claims demand still stronger evidence. This sequence guards against a common error. The printing of a million pamphlets may demonstrate capacity and expenditure, but it says nothing by itself about how many were read, believed or used in deciding whether to support the war. Source survival can further distort judgement because government production files are often easier to preserve than private reactions. Silence in an archive does not prove indifference, while a vivid hostile letter does not establish majority opinion. Claims about reach should state the denominator, and claims about reception should draw on varied evidence from different places, social positions and degrees of exposure.

America’s First Public Diplomacy Agency?

Calling the CPI America’s first public diplomacy agency is defensible only under a carefully specified definition. It was not the first American effort to engage people abroad. Benjamin Franklin had connected official negotiation with scientific, social and press networks in eighteenth-century France; nineteenth-century exhibitions had projected national images; and private educational and humanitarian organisations had built cross-border relationships. If first means the earliest contact with a foreign public, the claim fails. If it means the first large-scale federal body dedicated to coordinating numerous media and intermediaries around common international and domestic aims, the committee has a much stronger claim. Four tests clarify the issue: Was engagement with non-governmental audiences an explicit mission? Did a federal centre coordinate specialised staff and media? Did activity extend beyond a single diplomatic encounter? Did the organisation possess a durable statutory mandate? The CPI meets the first three more readily than the fourth. Stating the tests turns a celebratory title into a contestable historical argument.

The term public diplomacy itself was not the CPI’s official label in the contemporary institutional sense. Creel and his colleagues worked with the vocabularies of publicity, information and propaganda available during the war. Retrospective concepts can illuminate patterns, but they can also conceal differences. Later public diplomacy organisations operated under different laws, distinguished foreign from domestic audiences in different ways and often claimed peacetime educational or cultural purposes. A sound comparison therefore asks about mandate, authority, target public, reciprocity, time horizon and oversight. Analytical resemblance should not be confused with institutional identity. Historians can use a later category heuristically if they first reconstruct contemporary language and then explain the comparison. That sequence avoids assuming that Creel’s organisation shared later commitments to listening, exchange or peacetime relationship-building. It also prevents the morally loaded word propaganda from ending inquiry; the relevant questions concern disclosed source, evidence, coercive setting, feedback and room for disagreement.

Schindler’s question mark is consequently more useful than a simple declaration of priority. It directs attention to processes of institutional formation: which earlier practices were gathered together, what wartime urgency changed and why the arrangement proved difficult to sustain. The CPI’s novelty lay in the scale and centrality of coordination, the mobilisation of private and voluntary actors and the integration of several media. Its temporary character was equally important. Rather than searching for a single birth date of public diplomacy, students can trace how recurring practices became concentrated in one organisation and then dispersed again, leaving skills, precedents and political suspicion for later institutions. Institutional legacy requires a traceable carrier. Personnel moving into later work, reused distribution relationships, preserved manuals, copied procedures or explicit debate about the CPI would provide stronger evidence than a broad similarity of purpose. Discontinuity matters too: public suspicion of wartime propaganda could make later officials distance themselves from Creel’s model even while confronting comparable problems of coordination and credibility.

Effects, Limitations and Classroom Analysis

Assessing the CPI begins by separating institutional performance from persuasive effect. Budgets, staff numbers, pamphlets printed and speakers recruited show what the organisation could mobilise. Records of circulation show where messages travelled, while documented exposure shows that an audience had an opportunity to encounter them. Persuasion requires evidence that people interpreted a message in the intended way and altered an attitude or action because of it. These levels should not be collapsed. A surviving poster proves production; a cinema programme may prove exhibition; neither source can tell us by itself whether viewers trusted the claim or supported a particular policy. Each source must also be read for its institutional purpose. An annual report may maximise visible activity to defend the committee, while a hostile newspaper may select failures to attack it. Audience diaries, letters, local meeting minutes and later recollections offer different windows, but memory and self-presentation affect them too. Convergence among independent sources supports a cautious conclusion more effectively than one dramatic quotation.

Wartime conditions make causal inference especially difficult. Military events, economic pressures, patriotic rituals, presidential speeches, censorship and fear of social sanction all influenced behaviour. Support may have preceded exposure to CPI material, while apparent agreement may have reflected conformity rather than conviction. Researchers should compare groups, follow changes over time and search for records in which audiences explained their own responses. Even then, modest formulations are often most defensible. A campaign may have reinforced an existing frame, supplied language to supporters or narrowed the space for dissent without being the sole cause of mobilisation. The wider coercive environment must be distinguished from the CPI’s own formal authority without being excluded from analysis. Wartime law, surveillance and community pressure could alter how a supposedly persuasive appeal was received. A listener’s public assent may therefore indicate fear, patriotism, agreement or strategic silence. Evidence about private reasoning and timing is essential before attributing a changed action to communication.

In class, students analyse a CPI poster, a Four Minute Men script and a foreign newspaper item. For each source they record producer, intended public, intermediary, immediate purpose and expected response. They then place every available fact on an evidence ladder: production, distribution, exposure, interpretation, attitude and behaviour. The exercise requires them to mark where the chain breaks and name one additional source that could repair it. This procedure turns visual or rhetorical interpretation into a historical claim with limits. It also shows why knowing what an institution said is not equivalent to knowing what a public heard, and why hearing does not establish belief. A second round asks students to reverse their first interpretation by seeking plausible disconfirming evidence. The poster may never have been displayed, the speech may have been altered, or the newspaper may have printed the item to criticise it. Students then rewrite their claim with an explicit confidence level and identify which conclusion remains warranted despite the uncertainty.

Agency Design, Accountability and Democratic Tension

The CPI invites a broader design problem: how can a democratic government explain a war without treating citizens and foreign publics as objects to be managed? Some central coordination may be necessary to release information, answer hostile claims and prevent agencies from contradicting one another. Yet concentration of communication power can reduce plural debate, reward flattering accounts and make error correction politically costly. The committee’s volunteer network sharpened this tension. Familiar local speakers could make information accessible, but their social position could also make dissent visible and risky. Organisational effectiveness and democratic legitimacy must therefore be assessed together rather than treated as separate subjects. Source disclosure, documented standards of evidence, correction procedures and independent scrutiny are not obstacles external to effective communication; they shape credibility over time. Emergency speed may justify shortened review, but it does not erase the need to record decisions or set an expiry. A democratic institution must preserve space for criticism even when officials regard unity as strategically valuable.

A design workshop can make the choices concrete. Student teams create an agency with five boxes: mission, target public, partner network, feedback system and accountability mechanism. They must decide who authorises messages, how facts are verified, whether sources are disclosed and how criticism from local partners reaches decision-makers. They also specify an end date or process for reviewing emergency powers. Each choice creates a trade-off. Rapid clearance may improve responsiveness but weaken scrutiny; extensive approval may protect accuracy but leave rumours unanswered. The aim is not to produce a flawless institution, but to show how mandate and procedures shape the communication that audiences eventually receive. Teams should test their design with three failures: a false claim released under time pressure, a partner who changes the message and a foreign audience that reads it as hypocrisy. For each case they identify who learns, who corrects the record and who can impose a remedy. Stress-testing exposes whether feedback has actual authority or merely collects reactions for headquarters.

The final evaluation returns to Schindler’s historical argument. The CPI demonstrates that public diplomacy capacity can be assembled from government offices, commercial media, civic organisations and individual intermediaries. That hybridity expanded reach but dispersed control and responsibility. Its achievements should not be inferred from volume, and its ethical weaknesses should not be explained by the word propaganda alone. Researchers need evidence about institutional rules, partner behaviour, audience reception and alternative causes. Under that standard, the CPI is most valuable neither as an uncomplicated founding success nor as a uniquely manipulative exception, but as a revealing experiment in the possibilities and dangers of centralised wartime engagement. Its legacy is consequently double. It displayed the speed and reach obtainable through central coordination, while showing how wartime urgency, mixed audiences and delegated distribution strain accuracy and accountability. Later institutions could inherit techniques without inheriting the committee itself, and could learn from public suspicion as well as operational precedent. That qualified conclusion fits evidence better than either a founding myth or total dismissal.

Research seminar

Research question

By which criterion can the Committee on Public Information (CPI) be called the first public diplomacy agency, and what are the limits of that ‘first’ claim in light of earlier multi-actor practices?

Competing interpretations

Compare the unit of analysis and threshold used by an account that takes centralized, legally established organization as the starting point with those used by an account that finds older continuities in public–private cooperation and engagement with foreign publics.

Source criticism

Code the report of the Committee on Public Information's Division of Foreign Press in FRUS Public Diplomacy, document 12, for its claims about mandate, personnel, partnerships, and distribution. Do not read the institution's own report as foreign readers' response or proven effect.

Seminar assignment

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, state two explicit criteria for ‘agency’ and show how applying each changes the conclusion about the CPI. Distinguish FRUS document 12 from the editorial account of its creation in FRUS document 1 by source type. For the rival hypothesis that war explains exceptional administrative coordination, propose one observation to seek before the war and one after it.

Synthesis

  1. The CPI was established in 1917 as a temporary federal committee coordinating wartime communication activities.
  2. It used a multichannel network comprising the press, film, posters, speeches and local intermediaries.
  3. The CPI’s innovation was not the first engagement with public opinion, but the centralised, large-scale coordination of such activity.
  4. The number of materials produced or distributed is not direct evidence of persuasion or behavioural change.
  5. It is useful to study the CPI within the framework of public diplomacy, but historical concepts must not be used anachronistically.

Questions for discussion

  1. What criteria should be used to classify an organisation as the first public diplomacy agency?
  2. Did the CPI’s volunteer network lend credibility to the government’s message, or did it intensify social pressure?
