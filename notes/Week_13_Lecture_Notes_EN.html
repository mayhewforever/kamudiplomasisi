The main idea in a minute

A country’s news service, language course and student exchange do different things. Broadcasting may inform a large audience; a course may create repeated local contact; an exchange brings people into a shared experience. Ask “which arrangement might work, for whom and under what conditions?” rather than “which country is best?”

Think of an everyday example

Hypothetically, compare watching cooking videos, attending weekly classes and working alongside a cook. View counts and classroom friendships need different measures. First decide what you want to learn and how much time you have.

What will you learn?

  • Distinguish a tool from an operating model.
  • Ask different institutions the same clear questions.
  • Separate cultural appreciation from political support.

Start with these four terms

01Tool
A specific channel or activity used for contact.
Example: A social media account can publish announcements or make room for discussion.
02Model
The arrangement governing leadership, partner involvement and continued contact.
Example: The same language course can give local teachers decision rights or receive content only from headquarters.
03Autonomy
The space an institution or partner has to make its own decisions.
Example: Investigate whether a broadcaster can produce reports critical of its sponsor.
04Reciprocity
Both sides can influence the agenda and delivery, rather than simply listen.
Example: Course changes responding to local participants’ suggestions may indicate this.

Understand it step by step

  1. 1
    Choose a comparable question

    Replace “which is bigger?” with “which builds regular professional contact?” Examine the same period and similar participant groups. Do not directly rank budgets covering different activities.

  2. 2
    Examine the arrangement

    Broadcasters such as BBC World Service and education-cultural bodies such as the British Council offer different contact. A name or national label is insufficient: who funds it, selects content and allows local partners to change it?

  3. 3
    Listen beyond the institution

    Investigate participant experience alongside institutional reports. Enjoying a language course does not mean endorsing the sponsoring state’s policy. Better-informed participants may develop more detailed criticism; criticism alone is not failure.

Let's work through an example

Audience size or a continuing relationship?

Hypothetical exercise: institution A publishes an online talk; B runs a small joint workshop. You want to investigate later professional cooperation.

  1. Record views and workshop attendance as separate activity records.
  2. Ask both groups about joint projects or continuing contact three months later.
  3. Record prior relationships and nonresponses; do not immediately attribute differences to the institutional model.

The appropriate measure answers your question; it is not simply the institution’s largest-looking number.

A common mistake

“Copying a successful country’s institution will produce the same result.”

What to remember

Language, safety, local partners and decision freedom may differ. Investigate the conditions needed for an arrangement to work elsewhere.

Check your understanding

Why should two institutions from one country not be treated as one voice?

Show the answer and explanation

Their purposes, decision arrangements, forms of contact and participants may differ.

Explain · examine · apply

A closer academic reading

Comparative public diplomacy does more than rank institutions by visibility or budgets. It investigates how apparently similar activities acquire different meanings and consequences under different conditions. This week turns historical patterns into testable questions and examines cultural institutes, broadcasters, exchanges and digital networks as different operating arrangements. The aim is to explain which mechanisms can travel under which conditions, not nominate a universally superior country. Common questions must preserve differences in local history and recipient experience.

01

From historical patterns to research questions

Why this matters

A recurring practice is not an unchanging law of success.

Schindler’s historical approach identifies foreign public engagement before modern terminology. That continuity is the book’s overall finding, not one of its five concluding patterns. Those patterns concern communication, engagement methods, people and organisations, public-private partnerships, and publics in international relations. They organise inquiry without establishing in advance that an activity worked.

Convert a pattern into an explanatory proposition by specifying a mechanism, condition and possible counterevidence. A mechanism describes the intermediate processes through which an effect might arise. Local partners’ linguistic and relational knowledge may improve understanding, provided they have genuine decision space. Records repeatedly rejecting local advice would weaken this proposed explanation.

Consider two readings together. A continuity interpretation emphasises methods recurring under new institutional arrangements. A contextual interpretation emphasises similar methods serving different purposes and publics. The same instrument can persist while its meaning changes. Comparison should explain that change instead of using similar labels as evidence of unchanged outcomes.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical pattern test

Suppose both programmes work with local associations. In one, the association can change the agenda; in the other, it only recruits participants. Both may qualify as public-private partnerships without sharing the same mechanism. Investigate decision rights and resource sharing before claiming equivalence.

Pause and reason

How does a pattern differ from a success score?

Show the reasoning

It proposes questions and connections to investigate; it does not establish an effect or its size beforehand.

02

Comparability comes before numbers

Why this matters

Counting different things under one heading creates precise-looking but misleading results.

Conceptual equivalence means observations carry sufficiently similar meanings to answer a common question. Public diplomacy budgets covering different activities are not directly comparable. One may include scholarships and broadcasting while another covers only a communications unit. Currency conversion does not fix coverage differences; first establish which functions and periods the totals represent.

Structured comparison asks common questions: who defines the purpose, which public is addressed, who is absent, who controls resources, and what can local intermediaries change? Common questions constrain choosing measures favourable to a preferred case. They should not become an assumption that local concepts and experiences mean exactly the same thing everywhere.

Countries, institutions, programmes and individuals are different levels of analysis. Extending findings from one institute to a whole national model changes the level without justification. National popularity likewise does not establish a course’s effect. Specify the unit of the conclusion: which institutional programme, in which location, among which participants and over which period?

Worked illustration

Hypothetical budget comparison

Country A reports scholarships, cultural centres and broadcasting; country B reports only foreign-ministry communication costs. A larger total does not establish greater effort or a more effective model. Isolate common functions, then explain local partner contributions and accounting periods before interpreting differences.

Pause and reason

Why is a common currency insufficient?

Show the reasoning

It does not align the activities covered; different contents can still be counted in the same monetary unit.

03

Cultural institutes as relationship infrastructure

Why this matters

Visible events do not establish relationship quality or continuity.

Cultural institutes can translate national presence into local encounters through language, arts and education. Classifying the British Council and Goethe-Institut together does not imply identical governance. Legal position, funding, appointments and programme choices can differ. Investigate who can change which decisions instead of relying on an institution’s description of itself as autonomous.

Language learning may reduce the cost of future communication; artistic collaboration may sustain cooperation despite disagreement. These are conditional mechanisms. Local teachers and participants can make a programme reciprocal when they influence content. Distributing centrally prepared material may generate visibility while missing local needs. Equal event totals cannot distinguish these operating arrangements.

Follow who returns, whether partnerships survive staff turnover and whether criticism affects decisions. Continuity can be encouraging, yet people excluded by cost, location or language remain invisible. Investigate participation boundaries alongside the experiences of those inside. A popular cultural activity should not be equated with support for the sponsoring country’s foreign policy.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical course evaluation

Students enjoy a language course, but local teachers cannot propose programme changes. Preserve the satisfaction finding while recognising that it does not establish reciprocal agenda-setting or institutional autonomy. Teachers’ decision space is a separate evaluation question from student experience.

Pause and reason

What does a full course not prove?

Show the reasoning

Reciprocity, openness to criticism or endorsement of the sponsoring state’s foreign policy.

04

Investigating broadcaster credibility

Why this matters

Reach, trust and political support require distinct evidence.

International broadcasters can maintain frequent contact at scale through news and analysis. BBC World Service and Deutsche Welle illustrate an arrangement different from cultural institutes’ interpersonal relationships. A government may value national visibility while an audience values otherwise unavailable or more reliable information. These interests can overlap without being identical.

Editorial distance concerns how far news decisions can be separated from political instruction. Source attribution, distinguishing news from commentary, correcting errors and reporting inconvenient developments are observable signs. Assess repeated practice under difficult conditions rather than one favourable example. An institutional independence statement cannot replace evidence of actual decisions.

Signal availability, verified listening and recipient interpretation are different stages. Regular audiences may listen because they trust a service, but also to monitor an opponent, learn a language or find entertainment. These alternatives require separate measures of use and trust. Local distributors and platforms also shape the context in which content is encountered.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical puzzle about use

An interviewee regularly watches a foreign service “to see what they will say.” This establishes use, not trust. Ask separately which reports the person considers accurate and how they check information elsewhere. Viewing frequency alone cannot identify these different interpretations.

Pause and reason

Why can editorial distance not be read from an institution’s name?

Show the reasoning

Names and status do not reveal decisions. Examine appointments, content choices, corrections and responses to intervention.

05

Who makes relationships in exchanges and networks?

Why this matters

Participants are not passive carriers of a sender’s message.

Exchange places people inside relationships rather than simply before messages. Learning depends on selection, preparation, host experience, discussion freedom and continuing contact. Attributing relationships in multilateral programmes such as Erasmus+ to one state is especially difficult. Participants encounter multiple institutions and individuals and may interpret each connection differently.

Selection produces an important rival explanation. If internationally oriented people join a programme, favourable later attitudes cannot be attributed entirely to it. Record earlier orientations and networks. Alumni attaining influential positions also does not establish programme influence: seek evidence that a programme relationship entered an actual decision or collaborative activity.

Digital networks can connect distant participants, but connection counts do not establish quality. Network analysis maps who bridges separate groups; other evidence is needed to determine whether those ties are safe, reciprocal or useful. Platform ranking, data collection and harassment introduce additional intermediaries and risks. Network growth is not equivalent to increased participant decision power.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical alumni network

An alumni list has many records, but joint projects continue only within an old friendship group. Treating the list as relationship success hides earlier origins. Separate new relationships from existing ties and investigate excluded participants’ experiences. Having a dataset does not establish an inclusive network.

Pause and reason

Does criticism of the sponsoring country mean failure?

Show the reasoning

Not necessarily. If the purpose is learning and mutual understanding, better-informed criticism may fit it. Evaluate against the stated purpose.

06

How can a mechanism travel to another country?

Why this matters

Copying an organisational form does not reproduce its results.

Distinguish instruments, organisational forms and mechanisms. A language course is an instrument; an institute with specified decision rights is an organisational form; repeated contact reducing uncertainty is a mechanism claim. The mechanism can travel more flexibly because it explains why something might work. It still requires conditions such as safety, language, resources and partner decision space.

Do not conclude with the vague statement that cultures differ. Specify which difference changes which connection. Visa difficulties can interrupt contact, political pressure can restrict criticism, and language knowledge can affect comprehension. Context then becomes an observable part of the explanation rather than a warning attached after the argument is complete.

Conclusions should identify both benefits and risks. Specify which publics appear to benefit and who bears possible harm. Participants’ ability to object, partners’ ability to reshape objectives and avoiding unnecessary data collection belong inside transfer design. Under substantial uncertainty, a limited application followed by reassessment may be more defensible than rapid expansion.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical transfer decision

An institution wants to reproduce a promising joint workshop abroad. Local partners set its original agenda but cannot do so in the new location. Copying the event format does not preserve the relationship mechanism. Clarify decision rights first and make the expected result conditional on that arrangement.

Pause and reason

What is stronger than declaring one model best?

Show the reasoning

Explain which arrangement contributes to which result, for a specified public and conditions, while stating evidentiary limits.

Case workshop

Case workshop 1

Hypothetical case: A large budget, a better model?

One annual report includes headquarters and scholarships; another covers only local events. A student treats the larger total as stronger public diplomacy.

  1. Separate accounting coverage and identify functions present in both records.
  2. Specify the evaluation purpose. If it concerns local relationships, total expenditure alone is an unsuitable outcome measure.
  3. Seek attendance, return participation and joint decision records for a common period. Do not treat missing data as zero.
  4. Limit conclusions to comparable functions rather than the whole institution or national model.
A defensible conclusion

Establish conceptual equivalence first. More precise arithmetic cannot repair incompatible coverage.

Case workshop 2

Hypothetical case: Cultural interest and political criticism

Workshop participants want to return but criticise the sponsoring country’s foreign policy. A manager sees contradictory findings.

  1. Identify the stated purpose: cultural learning, professional relationships and political support are different objectives.
  2. Distinguish intentions to return from actual subsequent attendance; each needs different records.
  3. Examine criticism and its connection to learning without automatically classifying it as success or failure.
  4. Evaluate reciprocity separately by tracing whether local suggestions change the programme.
A defensible conclusion

Cultural interest can coexist with political criticism. Separate findings according to purpose and evidence.

Research practice

Ask two institutions the same questions

Use the shared workbook’s student evidence matrix to compare a broadcaster and a cultural institute. Label them hypothetical unless you have consulted actual sources.

  1. Write a common question and period. Check that different purposes are not compressed into one measure.
  2. Create separate entries for decision authority, local mediation, feedback and expected outcomes.
  3. Add source type, available observation and inference limits to each entry.
  4. In the 14-week guide, state two conditions required to transfer one practice elsewhere.
Read a model response

Hypothetical conclusion: the broadcaster offers regular information access while the institute provides repeated interpersonal contact. Mutual trust claims require recipient research in both cases. Similar results from the institute model cannot be assumed without local decision space and safe criticism.

Questions worth asking

Is speaking of a national model wrong?

It can be an initial classification, but should not conceal differences in decisions, funding and contact among institutions. Explanations should reach the specified programme where possible.

Are numbers unhelpful in comparison?

They help when scope, period and definitions align. Numbers inform a comparison whose equivalence has already been established; they do not create equivalence themselves.

What does recipient agency mean?

The ability to interpret, reject, reuse content and try to change a programme. Recipients are not merely the final destination carrying a sender’s intentions.

The academic argument

Week 13: Compare different models fairly

We will compare patterns from American history through British and German cultural institutes and broadcasters, then ask what further evidence wider transfers require.

Five Historical Patterns in Schindler's Cases

Schindler's history challenges the convenient story that American public diplomacy suddenly appeared when a modern agency or legal label was created. Continuity before the terminology is the book's overarching finding, not one of the five patterns named in its concluding synthesis. The first pattern is communication: actors repeatedly confronted the need to address foreign publics, changes in communication technology and weak links between headquarters and the field. The second is methods of engagement. Publications, personal advocacy, educational work, exchanges, relief, broadcasting and cultural activity reappeared in new combinations. A method could persist while its organisation, audience, technology and strategic purpose changed, so recurrence should not be mistaken for an identical programme or effect.

The third pattern is the importance of people and organisations that carry knowledge across institutional breaks. Diplomatic officers, educators, journalists, foundations, voluntary associations and commercial partners preserved contacts and operating skills even when government offices closed. The fourth is public-private partnership: private initiative often supplied access and credibility, while government offered diplomatic authority, resources and coordination, without making their aims identical. The fifth is the role of the public in international relations. Foreign publics were not merely scenery or passive recipients; their interpretations, networks and responses could shape the diplomatic environment in which officials and other actors made choices. This does not mean that public opinion mechanically determined policy. It means that an exclusively state-to-state account leaves out an actor and a set of pressures that Schindler's cases make historically visible.

These patterns are analytical propositions, not scores of success. An intention is not an activity; an activity is not its output; an output does not establish technical availability or potential reach; availability does not prove verified exposure; exposure does not reveal reception; favourable reception does not establish an intermediate outcome or decision; and a decision or behaviour is not automatically a durable impact. Schindler's historical approach is valuable because it makes this chain visible over time. It also warns against judging past programmes only through the organisations' own records. A press release can establish what an actor wanted to say, a distribution list can establish circulation and correspondence can reveal some reception, but claims about trust, behaviour or policy require different evidence. The patterns organise questions; they do not supply answers in advance. Treat the five patterns as middle-range hypotheses rather than timeless laws. A security crisis may accelerate activity, but can also centralise budgets or exclude criticism. Institutional inheritance supplies capacity while carrying old categories into a new problem. Public-private cooperation contributes expertise and distribution yet creates conflicts of interest. A new medium expands access without automatically producing credibility, and a local intermediary adapts a message while potentially changing its meaning. For each pattern, students should state a mechanism, a scope condition and a potentially disconfirming observation. This converts historical narrative into testable comparison without forcing every case into a single sequence. Patterns may appear together while having different weight, order and interaction in each country; comparison should explain that variation rather than erase it.

What Can Travel Beyond the American Case?

Historical transfer begins with a disciplined question: are we transferring an instrument, an organisational form or a causal mechanism? Copying an exchange programme is an instrument-level transfer. Creating a semi-autonomous cultural institute is an organisational transfer. Expecting repeated professional contact to reduce uncertainty and enable cooperation is a mechanism-level claim. The third is usually the most portable, because it explains why an intervention might work without assuming that its original institutional shell will fit elsewhere. Even mechanisms have scope conditions. Repeated contact will not necessarily build trust when participants lack safety, when selection is viewed as partisan, when visas interrupt continuity or when wider policy behaviour contradicts the relationship the programme claims to value.

The United States is therefore a source of hypotheses, not a universal template. Its federal political culture, suspicion of centralised propaganda, strong philanthropic sector, commercial media system, immigration history and shifting global position shaped the public-private arrangements described by Schindler. Another country may have a more centralised state, a different colonial memory, fewer independent foundations, a multilingual domestic public or a media environment in which an officially funded institution is interpreted differently. Regime type matters, but it is not the only contextual variable. Bilateral history, linguistic proximity, educational demand, security relationships, platform access and local memories of intervention can alter both the meaning of an activity and the evidence audiences regard as credible.

A structured comparison asks the same questions of each case. Who defines the problem? Which public is addressed, and who is absent from that category? What institution authorises and funds the activity? Which local intermediaries select, translate or contest it? What response can travel back to decision-makers? Which outcome is expected, over what period, and what rival explanation could produce the same observation? These questions prevent superficial comparison based on logos, budgets or event counts. They also make asymmetry visible. Two institutions may both teach a language, yet differ in curriculum control, partner autonomy and tolerance of criticism. The proper conclusion may be conditional: a mechanism appears transferable under specified conditions, while its organisational expression should be redesigned. Cross-national comparison requires conceptual equivalence before numerical precision. One country may count institutes, international broadcasting and scholarships in a public diplomacy budget, while another reports only a foreign ministry communications line. Currency conversion cannot repair that classification difference. Begin with functions, dates, accounting treatment and contributions from local partners. Regime type should also be disaggregated into observable dimensions such as media freedom, legislative oversight, executive centralisation and civil-society autonomy. Structured, focused comparison asks the same core questions of every case while retaining local concepts and histories. It replaces the vague search for the best national model with a conditional question: which arrangement can generate a specified result, through which mechanism, for which public and under what political constraints?

Cultural Institutes as Relationship Infrastructure

Cultural institutes turn an abstract national presence into repeated, local encounters. The British Council works through education, arts and language; the Goethe-Institut, Institut français, Instituto Cervantes and Yunus Emre Institute connect language learning with cultural programming in different institutional forms. The Japan Foundation supports cultural exchange, Japanese-language education and intellectual dialogue, while the Korea Foundation emphasises cultural and academic exchange, policy dialogue and international networks. Confucius Institutes provide a carefully bounded Chinese comparison: because they operate through host-institution partnerships, contractual transparency, academic autonomy and local governance are central questions, and arrangements should be assessed case by case. None of these bodies is simply an identical branch of a foreign ministry. Their legal position, funding, operational distance from government and openness to local co-production vary, shaping the credibility, continuity and adaptability of their work.

The activity is easy to see: a course, exhibition, translation workshop, school partnership or professional seminar. The intended mechanism is less visible. Language study can lower the cost of future contact; artistic collaboration can create a setting in which disagreement remains possible; translation can introduce ideas to new interpretive communities; and a long-running centre can become dependable relationship infrastructure during political tension. None of these pathways guarantees attraction. A programme chosen without local consultation may be read as self-promotion, and a popular event may generate enjoyment without changing views of the sponsoring state. Reception also differs within the same city: students, cultural professionals, officials and marginalised groups may encounter the institution in unequal ways.

Evaluation must therefore follow relationships rather than count only attendance. Useful evidence includes who returns, which partnerships survive staff turnover, whether local organisations help set the agenda, how participants describe the source's credibility and whether contacts later support joint decisions. Negative evidence matters as well: cancelled partnerships, repeated complaints, inaccessible pricing or the quiet exclusion of critical voices may reveal weaknesses hidden by large visitor totals. Comparison should also ask whether the institute learns. When feedback changes programme design, engagement becomes reciprocal at least in procedure; when criticism is collected but never reaches those with authority, listening remains symbolic. The outcome is not simply cultural visibility but the quality, distribution and practical use of the relationships created. Cultural institutes are not interchangeable copies. The Alliance Française originated in 1883 as a civil association, the British Council was founded in 1934, and the Goethe-Institut developed from 1951 in West Germany's post-war cultural relations. Their governance and funding evolved, and local branches do not all have the same relationship with the centre. Arm's-length status must be assessed through appointments, budget renewal, staffing, programme choice and the capacity to resist political instruction. Local teachers, curators and participants can make a programme reciprocal; merely exporting centrally prepared content can reduce an institute to a showcase. Enrolment is an output, a sustained professional network an intermediate outcome, and foreign-policy preference a distant, multi-causal result. Keeping those categories separate permits evaluation without reducing cultural value to immediate persuasion.

International Broadcasters and the Credibility Mechanism

International broadcasters operate through a different but overlapping model. BBC World Service, Deutsche Welle, France Médias Monde and NHK World-Japan address audiences through news, analysis and other programming across languages and platforms. Their public connections and governance structures differ, so the label international broadcasting does not tell us how editorial decisions are made. The strategic value claimed by governments often rests on reach and national presence, whereas the audience value of a broadcaster may rest on reliability, professional standards and access to information unavailable elsewhere. This creates a productive tension: editorial distance can make a publicly funded service more useful to the state precisely because audiences do not experience every report as an instruction from that state.

The mechanism begins with availability but does not end there. A signal or online feed must be accessible; editors and distributors must make it discoverable; audiences must recognise the format; and reports must survive comparison with lived experience and competing accounts. Credibility is cumulative and fragile. A broadcaster may gain trust by acknowledging unfavourable developments, distinguishing news from commentary and correcting errors. It may lose trust when audiences detect selective omission or when a sudden policy demand overrides established practice. Local journalists, retransmission partners, search systems and social platforms mediate reception, so central producers never control the final context. The broadcast or feed is an output; an accessible signal indicates technical availability or potential reach. Verified exposure requires evidence that people actually encountered it, and neither exposure nor audience size establishes interpretation, persuasion or a later decision.

Comparing BBC World Service with the British Council, or Deutsche Welle with the Goethe-Institut, shows why institutions from the same country should not be collapsed into a single national voice. Broadcasters usually offer frequent mediated contact at scale; cultural institutes more often organise slower interpersonal or educational relationships. The first may inform an audience that never meets the sender, while the second may build dense ties among a smaller group. Both can support public diplomacy, but through different causal routes and with different ethical duties. A fair assessment asks whether audiences used the information, whether credibility persisted across controversial coverage and whether exposure entered a later judgement. It does not infer policy support from audience size. International broadcasting earns credibility not by having no national connection, but by producing verifiable journalism despite that connection. The BBC's external tradition began with the Empire Service in 1932 and later changed name and remit; Deutsche Welle began broadcasting in 1953. Their legal and financial arrangements differ. Source attribution, separation of news and comment, visible correction, coverage inconvenient to the sponsor and resistance to editorial intervention are observable parts of a credibility mechanism. Regular use is not identical to trust: audiences may tune in to monitor an opponent, learn a language or seek entertainment. Research should measure perceived accuracy, independence and correctability separately. In a crisis, the trade-off between speed and verification can test in one news cycle a reputation accumulated over many years.

Exchanges, Networks and the Digital Comparison Lab

Exchange programmes place participants inside a relationship rather than merely in front of a message. Their theory is not that travel automatically creates admiration. Selection, preparation, host experience, freedom of discussion and continued contact shape what participants learn and whom they trust. Erasmus+ offers a multilateral European example: mobility and institutional networks may create durable ties without proving political alignment or an effect attributable to one state. Alumni may later become bridges between institutions, but they may also criticise a sponsoring country or institution with greater knowledge. That criticism need not indicate failure when the declared purpose is mutual understanding or professional connection. Claims about influence require evidence that the relationship entered an actual organisational or policy decision, not simply that an alumnus later held a prominent position.

Digital and network approaches extend these relational possibilities while changing their risks. Embassies, institutes, broadcasters, universities and civil-society partners can convene communities across borders, invite rapid feedback and connect participants who never share a physical site. Yet platforms rank visibility, retain data and encourage short cycles of attention. A high interaction count can reflect controversy, automation or entertainment rather than trust. Network analysis can show who connects otherwise separate groups, but it cannot by itself reveal the quality or ethics of the connection. A programme becomes genuinely networked when partners share some agenda-setting power and feedback can alter decisions; distributing centrally prepared content through many accounts remains amplification, not co-production.

In the comparison lab, students will examine one cultural institute, one international broadcaster and one exchange or network programme. Each team will map activity, output, technical availability or potential reach, verified exposure, reception and interpretation, intermediate outcome, decision or behaviour and longer-term impact, then identify the missing evidence between stages. It will code governance, editorial or programme autonomy, local mediation, feedback, time horizon and ethical risk. The final judgement must separate what the institution produced, who actually encountered it, how selected publics interpreted it and what wider consequence can reasonably be attributed to it. Schindler's five patterns become questions about communication, methods of engagement, people and organisations, public-private partnerships and the role of publics in international relations. They remain open to revision when a non-American case reveals a different mechanism or a limit to transfer. Exchanges and digital networks should be compared through duration, intensity and participant selection. A face-to-face scholarship is costly but immersive; an online community can reach many people while sustaining thinner involvement. Both attract participants who may already be internationally oriented, so favourable post-programme attitudes require a baseline and a credible comparison. An alumni list becomes relational infrastructure only when members exchange resources, build projects and can voice criticism. Digital platforms may sustain contact, but algorithms, privacy and harassment introduce new intermediaries and risks. Students should compare two countries using the same purpose, audience, governance, mediator, time horizon and evidence categories. The strongest conclusion is conditional—for example, repeated contact plus local programme autonomy may make durable professional ties more likely—rather than a ranking that declares one entire national model superior.

Research seminar

Research question

Can differences in governance between a cultural institute and an international broadcaster independently explain credibility despite their connections to the state?

Competing interpretations

Compare an interpretation that treats formal editorial and administrative autonomy as decisive with one that gives greater explanatory weight to everyday practice, funding, and local partnerships; do not equate institutional self-description with actual outcomes.

Source criticism

In the current text of the BBC Royal Charter on the reading list, code provisions on the organization's purposes and independence. This primary legal document does not show actual BBC editorial decisions or foreign viewers' trust; the Goethe-Institut and British Council ‘About Us’ pages are also institutional self-descriptions.

Seminar task

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, compare a provision on authority or independence from the BBC Royal Charter with one governance claim in the Goethe-Institut or British Council self-description. Use Cull's classification of instruments as a shared analytical language. Design a decision record or reception record that could test the rival account of political influence in practice despite formal guarantees.

Synthesis

  1. Access, mediation, credibility, adaptation and feedback are mechanisms that recur across different historical periods.
  2. Historical continuity does not mean an unchanging institution, but the reuse of particular resources and practices under new conditions.
  3. Broadcasting, cultural relations, relational networks and public-private models rest on different logics of success.
  4. Global examples cannot be ranked directly without considering their political regimes, histories, institutions and media contexts.
  5. Popularity, visibility and a large budget do not by themselves establish diplomatic effect.

Questions for discussion

  1. Which mechanisms of public diplomacy persist despite changes in historical period and technology?
  2. Why must cultural attraction be distinguished from support for a state's foreign policy?
  3. Which of the four models does Türkiye's public diplomacy structure most closely resemble?
