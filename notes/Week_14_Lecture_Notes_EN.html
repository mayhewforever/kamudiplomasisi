Evidence-based assessment of effectiveness

Analytical overview

Completed activities and changes in knowledge, trust or behaviour are distinct dimensions of evaluation. The final week translates success claims into bounded, answerable research questions and relates each to suitable evidence. Evaluation includes the collection of numerical data, explicit reporting of evidentiary gaps and protection of participants from harm.

Illustrative analogy

In a hypothetical example, a potted plant grows after a new fertiliser is applied. Growth may reflect the fertiliser, increased sunlight or regular watering. Subsequent growth is a temporal observation; attributing it causally to the fertiliser requires careful comparison.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish analytically between completed activities and observed change.
  • Examine the population forming a proportion’s denominator and the pattern of missing responses.
  • Develop a recommendation proportionate to the evidence and attentive to participant protection.

Key concepts and distinctions

01Output
The product or completed work directly generated by an activity.
Example: A published video is an output; it does not establish changed opinions.
02Outcome
The change in knowledge, relationships or behaviour being investigated.
Example: Retention of knowledge acquired in a workshop can be examined three months later.
03Baseline
A measurement recording the relevant situation before a programme begins.
Example: Assessment of learning requires establishing participants’ knowledge before the programme.
04Rival explanation
An alternative cause that could account for the observed change.
Example: Participants’ prior interest may limit the attribution of favourable opinions entirely to the programme.

Stages of analysis

  1. 1
    Bounding the research question

    A question about general improvement in national image has a broad scope. A hypothetical workshop evaluation could instead examine participants’ use of a method three months later. The population, change and observation period should be specified at the outset.

  2. 2
    Integrating appropriate evidence

    Attendance records document presence, knowledge questions assess recall, and interviews investigate interpretation of experience. These sources address different dimensions. Examine the baseline and a suitable comparison group while accounting for possible initial differences between groups.

  3. 3
    Incorporating limitations into decisions

    Weak evidence may warrant further information collection rather than a definitive success claim. Report nonresponse and possible harms. Participants should understand the research purpose and be able to express criticism; unnecessary personal data should not be collected.

Applied case analysis

Defining the scope of a percentage

Entirely hypothetical exercise: 40 people attended a workshop; 20 answered a follow-up survey, of whom 12 reported using the new method.

  1. Calculate reported use among respondents: 12 ÷ 20 = 60%.
  2. Do not generalise 60% to all participants; use among the 20 nonrespondents remains unknown.
  3. Investigate prior use and other training. Where feasible, compare reported use with evidence of application that requires no personal data collection.

The proportion describes reported use among respondents; it does not establish that the programme alone caused the observed change.

Common interpretative error

“Information accuracy guarantees that a campaign is ethical.”

Analytical clarification

Accurate information may be presented under pressure or accompanied by unnecessary personal data collection. Sources, purposes, refusal rights and risks of harm require separate assessment.

Conceptual assessment

Are favourable survey responses sufficient to establish a causal relationship?

Reasoned response

They are insufficient alone; prior attitudes, other events and nonrespondents’ experiences may provide alternative explanations.

Research application

Enter 40 participants, 20 responses and 12 reports of use in the shared Excel workbook’s hypothetical measurement lab. Calculate the proportion among respondents and record one limitation in the student evidence matrix.

Methodological guidance: Do not use actual student data. Add a single-sentence recommendation to the final section of the 14-week guide identifying which additional evidence could change the decision.

Concepts · evidence · interpretation

Extended academic analysis

The final week translates favourable programme narratives into testable evaluation questions. It examines whether change followed an activity and the mechanism through which the programme may have contributed. This requires conceptually appropriate measurement, explicit comparison assumptions and participant protection. Evaluation justifies the decisions supported by available evidence and identifies uncertainties that could alter them. This supplement supports the staged preparation of a final evidence file.

01

Logic models and theories of change

Analytical significance

A diagrammatic arrow identifies a relationship claim requiring explanation and testing; it is not evidence of that relationship.

A logic model organises programme resources, activities and expected changes. Funding and personnel are inputs; conducting a seminar is an activity; the completed seminar is a direct output. Technical availability concerns opportunities for encounter, whereas verified exposure establishes actual contact with content. Distinguishing these categories prevents the inference of recipient change directly from activity completion.

A theory of change explains why the proposed connections are expected to operate. In a hypothetical professional workshop, joint problem-solving may generate knowledge that participants adapt to their work and institutions permit them to apply. Each connection depends on specified conditions. Learning may occur without application if participants lack the necessary workplace authority. In that situation, absent application should not be attributed directly to teaching quality.

Developing the model backwards from the expected outcome makes implicit assumptions explicit. A joint project may require complementary expertise, time, institutional approval and continuing contact. Conditions within programme control must be distinguished from external conditions. The model’s analytical value depends on identifying testable connections, potential interruptions and unintended consequences, rather than the number of components it contains.

Illustrative application

Hypothetical interruption of the implementation mechanism

In the hypothetical example, participants learn a method but their institutions do not provide the required software. Improved knowledge and absent application are compatible findings about different stages. Investigation of resources or approval may be warranted before additional teaching. Identifying the interrupted connection allows the proposed intervention to address the relevant problem.

Analytical question

Which three elements should be specified for each connection in the model?

Reasoned response

The proposed mechanism, a necessary condition and an observation that would support or weaken the connection.

02

Assessing the relationship between concepts and indicators

Analytical significance

An easily counted observation may not represent the central concept in the research question.

An indicator is an observable sign selected to represent a concept that cannot be observed directly or requires summarisation. Trust is a concept; a question concerning a specified institution, whose meaning has been assessed, is a possible indicator. A count of likes records a different behaviour. Describing numerous likes as trust does not resolve the measurement problem; it obscures the conceptual mismatch.

Validity concerns representation of the intended concept; reliability concerns consistency of measurement under similar conditions. Consistent results do not exclude measurement of the wrong concept. Assessment should therefore examine the institution targeted by a question, participants’ interpretation of its wording and whether translation preserves its meaning, alongside repeatability. The same word may be associated with different expectations across groups.

Every numerical result requires specification of the observation unit, population covered, period and production rule. The denominator defines the group within which a proportion is calculated; respondents and all participants may differ. Averages may also conceal distributions, including benefits for some groups alongside exclusion of others. Missing responses should not be treated as zero; their causes and possible implications for interpretation require investigation.

Illustrative application

Hypothetical indicator correction

A hypothetical team reports event satisfaction under the heading “increased trust.” The question may have measured responses to the venue or presenter. The concept should first be narrowed and the question’s target specified. Data that do not support the trust claim can be retained under an appropriate conceptual heading; correcting measurement does not require deleting useful observations.

Analytical question

Is reliable measurement necessarily valid?

Reasoned response

Reliability alone does not establish validity. Consistent responses may measure a concept other than the trust under investigation.

03

Counterfactual comparison in causal inference

Analytical significance

Temporal order is necessary but does not independently establish causality.

Causal inference compares observed change with a plausible situation in the absence of the programme. This unobserved alternative is the counterfactual. The same individuals cannot experience the same period both participating and not participating. Research design must therefore justify how adequately the available comparison represents that alternative. Stating only that an event occurred after the programme does not provide this justification.

Selection concerns initial differences among participants; prior interest may account for subsequent favourable attitudes. A baseline makes some differences observable but does not eliminate them all. The existence of a comparison group does not independently establish its suitability. Exposure to different crises, policies or information sources may make attribution of observed change to the programme misleading.

Method selection should follow the research question and available evidence. Random assignment may be useful in some settings, although ethical or practical conditions can constrain its application. Matching on observed characteristics does not automatically eliminate unobserved differences. Comparing changes across two groups requires a defensible assumption of similar trends without the intervention. Naming a method does not replace explanation and testing of its assumptions.

Illustrative application

Hypothetical temporal-order error

In the hypothetical example, two institutions sign an agreement after an exchange programme, but earlier correspondence shows that negotiations began beforehand. This does not exclude a programme contribution, but weakens the claim that it initiated cooperation. Research should examine which subsequent stage the relationship may have facilitated.

Analytical question

Why are baseline and follow-up measurements insufficient alone?

Reasoned response

Other events during the same period may explain change. Comparison and mechanism evidence remain necessary.

04

Integrating different sources within an explanatory assessment

Analytical significance

Mixed methods requires analytical integration beyond supplementing numerical findings with quotations.

Surveys can investigate response distributions within a defined population, while interviews examine meanings and the processes through which responses develop. Observation can identify differences between reported and actual behaviour. Administrative records document implementation, while decision records indicate the use of information. Combining methods adds analytical value when answers to different questions jointly test an integrated explanation.

Process tracing investigates the expected intermediate stages connecting activity to decisions. A claim that an encounter facilitated cooperation may be examined through meeting records, subsequent communication, shared information and decision rationales. Documents differ in their ability to discriminate among explanations. A general expression of satisfaction compatible with several explanations may provide weaker evidence than a dated record demonstrating the use of specific information in a decision.

Disagreement among sources requires examination of timing, samples and question differences before any source is excluded. Surveys may indicate average improvement while interviews document exclusion within a particular group; both findings may be valid. Analysis should explain differing experiences without requiring convergence on a single conclusion. Source independence also matters: documents reproducing one manager’s report may provide no additional corroboration.

Illustrative application

Hypothetical resolution of disagreement

In the hypothetical example, surveys report useful learning experiences while observation shows that some participants could not enter the discussion. Learning and opportunities to contribute should be treated as separate concepts. Research can then examine who learned, who was excluded and which programme arrangements generated this pattern. Integrating the findings may support a more specific design recommendation.

Analytical question

Under which conditions can additional methods strengthen the evidence?

Reasoned response

The number of methods is not independently decisive. Each method’s question, data quality and contribution to testing explanations from other sources must be specified.

05

Ethical dimensions of programme and research design

Analytical significance

Accurate information does not answer every ethical question.

Persuasion preserves opportunities to identify the source, test a claim, express disagreement and refuse. Manipulation restricts independent judgement by concealing material information or exploiting vulnerability. Accurate content can also be presented through processes that conceal the source or prevent meaningful refusal. Ethical assessment therefore examines the conditions of the relationship alongside the accuracy of statements.

Research processes generate specific risks. Removing names does not necessarily protect identity: combined information about occupation, institution and location can identify individuals within small networks. Data minimisation means avoiding collection or retention unnecessary for the specified purpose. Possible future usefulness does not justify unrestricted data collection. Participants should understand which information is shared, why, with whom and for how long.

Reciprocity requires opportunities for local partners to participate beyond providing data. They should be able to challenge research questions, identify risks and receive findings in a usable form. Complaint procedures, access restrictions and deletion schedules are components of research design. Where scholarships or professional opportunities are involved, the voluntariness of consent requires specific examination; a signed form alone does not establish it.

Illustrative application

Hypothetical visibility risk

A hypothetical evaluation team removes a participant’s name from a success account but publishes a unique role and institutional affiliation. The combined information may continue to identify the person. Assessment must consider what the details reveal together and remove information unnecessary for the research purpose. Removing the name alone does not guarantee anonymity.

Analytical question

Why does research use of public posts require a separate risk assessment?

Reasoned response

Collecting and quoting public posts can increase visibility and create identification or harm that the person did not anticipate.

06

Developing an evidence-based, actionable recommendation

Analytical significance

Evaluation should produce a reasoned decision rather than remain limited to a general success classification.

Documented facts, findings supported by different sources, plausible inferences and unresolved questions should be distinguished. This classification identifies where interpretation enters the analysis. Confidence requires justification through data quality, evidentiary directness, source independence and the extent of testing rival explanations. Narrative coherence cannot establish a missing causal connection; the support for a claim and its limitations must both remain explicit.

Failure to detect an effect has several possible explanations: the programme may have been ineffective, the wrong concept may have been measured, the observation period may have been insufficient, or the research may have lacked the capacity to distinguish change. These alternatives should be expressed as testable predictions, each with an expected observation and evidence that could change the decision. Concealing adverse or unequal outcomes weakens institutional learning and accountability.

Recommendations should relate continuation, modification, limited expansion, suspension or termination to explicit reasons. They should specify the responsible actor, timetable, resources and early indicators to monitor. Weak evidence may justify limited implementation with stronger measurement. Numerous visible outputs cannot independently justify continuation in the presence of serious harm; the recommendation’s priorities should be explicit.

Illustrative application

Hypothetical cautious recommendation

A hypothetical programme operates regularly, but only satisfied participants respond to follow-up surveys. Limited continuation with investigation of missing responses and a stronger baseline may be recommended before expansion. Specifying in advance which findings would warrant expansion or redesign makes the recommendation more readily testable.

Analytical question

What purpose does retaining uncertainty serve in a reasoned evaluation conclusion?

Reasoned response

It identifies decision-relevant gaps, matches claims to evidence and directs further research towards a concrete question.

Applied case studies

Applied case studies 1

Hypothetical case: The group behind a percentage

In the introductory guide’s entirely hypothetical example, 20 of 40 participants responded to a survey and 12 reported using a method. This case examines the limits of inference from that proportion.

  1. State that the proportion concerns 12 among 20 respondents; do not apply it to all participants.
  2. Investigate missing responses. Avoidance by nonusers and lost contact information pose different problems.
  3. Ask about prior usage and other training that could explain it.
  4. Consider comparing reports with practical traces that do not expose participants to risk.
Analytical conclusion

The supported finding concerns reported use among respondents. Use across all participants and causal influence require more extensive evidence.

Applied case studies 2

Hypothetical case: The evidentiary scope of a joint project

Joint research begins after a hypothetical exchange programme. Participants report that the encounter was useful, while the institutions are known to have planned cooperation before the programme.

  1. Order the earlier plan, programme encounter and project decision chronologically.
  2. Identify which information gap or trust problem the encounter might have addressed.
  3. Seek that contribution in decision records rather than relying only on positive recollection.
  4. Treat the earlier institutional plan as a competing or complementary explanation.
Analytical conclusion

The programme may have facilitated a particular intermediate stage without independently producing the outcome. The contribution claim should be limited to the stage supported by evidence.

Research application

Preparing the final evidence file

Use the shared workbook’s student evidence matrix and hypothetical measurement lab together to prepare a bounded evaluation of one programme. Do not enter actual student data.

  1. Specify a public, period and change, and label the scenario hypothetical.
  2. Record observations, sources, missing information and ethical risks at each stage.
  3. State the strongest rival explanation and one item of evidence that could test it.
  4. Add a recommendation to the 14-week guide specifying action, justification and information that would change the decision.
Model response

Hypothetical conclusion: programme activity is established, and some participants report applying new knowledge. Initial differences and missing responses prevent a general impact claim. Limited continuation should be accompanied by stronger comparison, avoidance of unnecessary personal data collection and safe investigation of adverse experiences.

Conceptual and methodological questions

What can evaluation contribute when definitive causal attribution is unavailable?

Reasoned contribution analysis can identify supported mechanisms and rival explanations that remain plausible. The scope of the claim should correspond to the strength of the evidence.

Does an adverse finding require immediate programme termination?

Termination does not follow automatically. The finding requires examination in relation to measurement, implementation and harm, and the recommendation should address severity and the possibility of correction.

Why should data collection be limited by the research purpose?

Unnecessary data increases participant risk and the possibility of misuse. Information should be selected for its relevance to the research question, with retention, access and deletion arrangements defined in advance.

Academic argument

Evidence-based assessment of effectiveness

In the final week, we will consider how public diplomacy can be measured, the ethical limits within which it should operate and how to construct an evidence-based evaluation report.

From a Strategic Aim to a Testable Logic Model

Evaluation begins before a programme starts. A useful logic model names the public problem, the people affected, the actors able to intervene, the resources available and the assumptions linking action to change. A vague aim such as improving a country's image is not yet evaluable. It must be translated into a bounded proposition: for a specified public, a defined activity is expected to change a particular form of knowledge, trust, relationship or behaviour within a plausible period. Context belongs inside the model rather than in a final footnote. Media freedom, bilateral tension, language, access, security and the conduct of the sponsoring state's wider foreign policy can enable or block the proposed mechanism.

The model should separate inputs, activities, outputs, technical availability or potential reach, verified exposure, reception and interpretation, intermediate outcomes, decisions or behaviour and longer-term impact. Funding and staff are inputs; a seminar, broadcast or exchange is an activity; and the completed event, content or funded place is an output. Technical availability asks whether access was possible, while verified exposure requires evidence that people actually encountered the output. Reception concerns how they interpreted it. An intermediate outcome might be greater knowledge, trust or a new professional tie. Decision evidence asks whether such a change entered an individual, organisational or policy choice; impact is a more distant and durable development. A platform count of one thousand views establishes recorded circulation, not one thousand unique human exposures or trust, and a signed partnership may show a decision without proving that cooperation endured.

Working backwards from the desired outcome exposes hidden assumptions. If an exchange is expected to support joint research, participants must have complementary expertise, sufficient autonomy, a way to remain in contact and institutions willing to recognise the collaboration. Each assumption suggests an indicator and a possible failure point. The model should also identify unintended pathways: exclusion may deepen resentment, a partner may face political risk, or a message designed for one group may circulate to another. A good logic model is therefore not a decorative arrow chart. It is a contestable argument that states what should be observed if the mechanism operates, what would count against it and where programme managers have authority to adapt. A logic model makes a strategic slogan observable and contestable. Increasing mutual trust among young people is a worthwhile aim, but not an activity. A team identifies inputs such as money, expertise, partners, time and data access; activities such as workshops, exchange or an alumni network; outputs such as participation; short-term outcomes such as learning or contact quality; intermediate outcomes such as joint projects; and distant impact. Every arrow needs an assumption—for example, that participants listen to one another and consider the local partner credible—and a risk. Feedback, unintended effects and participant agency should be included rather than treating the programme as a pipe. A theory of change goes further by explaining why the proposed link should operate under specified conditions and what alternative pathways exist. The best model is not the busiest diagram, but the one that reveals which link is evidenced and which remains an assumption.

Indicators and a Mixed-Methods Measurement Strategy

An indicator is useful only when it represents the concept under investigation. Website visits document recorded traffic, but do not necessarily establish unique human exposure or comprehension; satisfaction may describe an event experience, but not trust; a new memorandum may indicate formal commitment, but not implementation. Evaluators should define each concept, choose an observable sign, specify whose experience is measured and decide when change should become visible. Disaggregation is essential. An average can conceal differences by age, region, language, gender, professional role or prior attitude. Targets also require a benchmark: change from a baseline, difference from a credible comparison or progress towards a stated threshold. Otherwise a large number may look impressive without revealing whether anything improved.

Mixed methods combine evidence that answers different questions. A survey can estimate the distribution of attitudes in a defined population; interviews can uncover how participants interpreted an encounter; focus groups can reveal shared language and disagreement; observation can show how an activity actually operated; media and discourse analysis can trace frames; network analysis can map relationships; digital records can document circulation; and archives can recover institutional intention and internal debate. These methods do not become rigorous merely by being combined. Sampling, question wording, translation, interviewer effects, missing data and unequal digital access must be considered. The purpose of combination is to test the mechanism from several angles, not to decorate a report with more data.

A practical measurement plan links each stage of the logic model to a source, timing and responsibility. Outputs and technical availability may be monitored continuously; verified exposure and reception may be sampled after contact; intermediate relational outcomes, decisions and longer-term impacts require appropriately later observations. Repeated measurement is often more informative than a single end-of-event form because it shows whether a change persists, fades or develops slowly. Administrative dashboards are valuable for management, but they should not turn convenient platform metrics into strategic outcomes. Qualitative findings can explain why a numerical trend appeared, while quantitative evidence can test whether an interview theme extends beyond a few articulate participants. When sources disagree, the evaluator should investigate the disagreement and preserve it in the conclusion rather than force false consistency. Indicators must not substitute what is easy to count for what matters. Reach may use unique users, location and completion; engagement may use substantive replies and return participation; learning may require baseline and follow-up knowledge; relationship quality may involve trust, reciprocity and joint action. Each measure needs a denominator, time window, source and disaggregation rule. Ten thousand likes are uninterpretable without the exposed population, while an average can conceal unequal experiences and missing responses. Quantitative evidence estimates scale and change; interviews illuminate meaning and mechanism; observation can reveal differences between reported and actual practice. Mixed methods require planned integration, not decorative quotations. Disagreement is informative: a survey may show average trust rising while interviews reveal exclusion among a minority. A metric sheet should record definition, formula, source, frequency, owner, subgroup variables and ethical risk.

Causal Inference and Rival Explanations

The causal question is not simply whether attitudes or behaviour changed after a programme. It is whether the programme contributed to that change, through the proposed mechanism, compared with what would plausibly have happened without it. This counterfactual cannot usually be observed directly. Public diplomacy unfolds in open political environments where elections, conflict, economic shocks, popular culture, personal experience and other international actors operate at the same time. Causal inference therefore depends on design and transparent reasoning rather than on a claim that one dataset speaks for itself. The strength of the conclusion should match the strength of the comparison, the quality of the process evidence and the degree to which alternatives have been tested.

Several designs can strengthen an inference. Baseline and follow-up measures establish sequence; a carefully chosen comparison group helps estimate whether a similar change would probably have occurred without the programme, subject to assumptions about comparability; repeated observations identify trends before and after an intervention; and process tracing searches for the expected steps connecting activity to decision. Random allocation can be useful in limited settings, but it is often impractical or ethically unsuitable for relationship programmes. A small case study can still support a strong claim when it finds distinctive evidence predicted by the mechanism, such as records showing that a trusted programme contact supplied information used in a later joint decision. Conversely, a broad correlation between programme exposure and favourable opinion remains weak if prior interest shaped who participated.

Rival explanations should be written before the final judgement. Selection effects arise when already favourable or internationally oriented people join a programme. Maturation and repeated testing can change responses over time. Social desirability may encourage participants to praise a sponsor, while attrition may remove dissatisfied respondents from follow-up. A diplomatic agreement, crisis or cultural trend may shift opinion independently; spillovers may expose the comparison group. Evaluators can rarely eliminate every rival, but they can ask what evidence each would predict and compare those predictions with the record. Null, delayed, unequal and harmful effects are findings, not embarrassments. The defensible conclusion may be contribution rather than sole causation, with a clearly stated level of confidence. Causal inference asks what would have happened without the programme. Random assignment may suit some digital content or phased invitations, but fairness, small samples, political sensitivity and spillovers often constrain it in diplomacy. Alternatives include matched comparison, difference-in-differences, interrupted time series, synthetic control, process tracing and contribution analysis. None is automatic: difference-in-differences needs a defensible parallel-trends assumption, matching cannot remove unobserved confounding, and time series remain vulnerable to simultaneous shocks. Process tracing searches for expected intermediate steps and evidence against rival explanations. Design should begin with how participation was allocated, when data exist and which confounder is most threatening, not with a fashionable method name. Sensitivity checks, analysis of attrition and a pre-specified plan improve credibility. Without strong causal identification, evidence consistent with a contribution is usually more accurate than a claim that the programme caused the outcome.

Ethics: Transparency, Reciprocity and Privacy

Ethics concerns both the programme and its evaluation. Accurate information is necessary, but it is not sufficient. Audiences should be able to identify the source, understand the purpose and distinguish independent editorial or academic judgement from official advocacy. Persuasion leaves room for verification, disagreement and refusal; manipulation conceals material facts or exploits vulnerability in ways that weaken autonomous judgement. Reciprocity asks whether foreign partners can shape the agenda, question assumptions and benefit on terms they recognise, rather than merely provide access or local legitimacy. Power asymmetry does not disappear because a programme uses the language of dialogue, so evaluators must examine who can say no and who carries the consequences of failure.

Measurement creates its own risks. Digital traces may reveal political interests, location or relationships even when a dataset lacks a person's name. Registration forms and alumni systems can accumulate information that is convenient to retain but unnecessary for the stated purpose. Ethical design uses informed and comprehensible consent, data minimisation, secure storage, limited access and a realistic deletion schedule. It also considers whether participation is genuinely voluntary when scholarships, visas or professional opportunities are involved. Publicly available posts are not automatically ethically free to collect, especially when quotation could expose a vulnerable person. Local partners should help identify risks that a distant evaluator may not recognise.

Ethical governance must remain active throughout the programme. A review process should assess targeting, source disclosure, partner safety, accessibility and possible unintended harm before launch. Participants need a usable complaint channel and, where feasible, the ability to withdraw their data without losing unrelated benefits. Findings should be returned in forms that partners can understand and use; extractive evaluation gathers local knowledge but shares neither interpretation nor value. Transparency also means reporting limitations and negative results, not only publishing favourable stories. An ethical recommendation may ask managers to change a method, narrow a target, add safeguards or stop an activity even when it produces visible outputs. Strategic utility does not cancel duties of care and accountability. Ethics covers purpose, means, relationships and the full data life cycle. Informed consent requires people to understand what is collected, why, with whom it will be shared and how long it will remain. Data minimisation rejects collecting every potentially useful variable; purpose limitation prevents later reuse for undisclosed targeting. Identification can endanger politically exposed, migrant or young participants, and nominal anonymisation may fail in a small network where roles are distinctive. Algorithmic targeting can quietly magnify exclusion or discrimination. Reciprocity requires returning findings accessibly and allowing participants to criticise a programme, not merely extracting their data. Independent review, a complaint route, a deletion schedule and a breach response plan belong to governance. Urgent political objectives do not cancel these safeguards. Treatment of people and their information shapes institutional trust, so an ethical failure is also a strategic failure.

Final Evidence Lab: From Claim to Recommendation

The final laboratory begins with a tightly bounded case: one programme, a specified public, a meaningful period and one principal impact claim. Teams will build a claim-evidence matrix. For intention and context, then for activity, output, technical availability or potential reach, verified exposure, reception and interpretation, intermediate outcome, decision or behaviour and longer-term impact, they will record what is known, the source, its limitations and the missing evidence. They will diagram the proposed mechanism and list the conditions it requires. Programme documents can establish mandate, activity and output; distribution and technical records can test availability; audience evidence can address exposure and interpretation; and decision records can show whether a relationship or idea entered practice. No source should be asked to prove more than it can.

Each team will then conduct a friendly challenge session. One member presents the strongest causal account, another presents the strongest rival explanation, and a third audits ethics and data quality. Claims are classified as documented fact, convergent finding, plausible inference, disputed interpretation or unresolved assertion. The team should identify one piece of evidence that would most change its judgement and explain whether it can realistically be obtained. This exercise rewards calibrated confidence rather than rhetorical certainty. A polished story with a broken evidence chain is weaker than a modest conclusion that shows exactly which link is supported, where uncertainty remains and how future evaluation could reduce it.

The final recommendation must be actionable and conditional: continue, modify, scale carefully, pause or end the programme, with reasons tied to evidence. It should specify which mechanism appears to work for whom, under what conditions, at what ethical and resource cost. If output and technical availability are strong but exposure or reception is unknown, the recommendation may be to improve audience research before expansion. If relationships are valuable but unequally distributed, access may need redesign. If a claimed policy impact rests on an untested rival explanation, the language should be narrowed. The course ends with one disciplined habit: never collapse activity, output, availability or potential reach, verified exposure, reception, intermediate outcome, decision or behaviour and longer-term impact into a single success narrative. Public diplomacy becomes professionally credible when learning and accountability are built into strategy. A final report should be a decision-ready evidence argument, not an archive of everything collected. The executive summary connects purpose, principal finding, confidence, major limitation and recommendation. Methods document the sample, comparison, dates, measures and analytical choices well enough to inspect. Findings separate outputs from outcomes, give every table a denominator and uncertainty, and retain null or adverse results. Discussion evaluates mechanism and rivals. A recommendation identifies the action, responsible unit, timing, resource need and early signal to monitor; weak evidence may justify a better pilot rather than immediate scale. Students can grade claims with a defined confidence scale derived from data quality, consistency, directness and design. The closing questions are simple but demanding: what does the evidence show, what does it not show, and what new information would change the decision? This approach manages uncertainty and makes evaluation useful for accountability, learning and more ethical practice.

Research seminar

Research question

How can the ‘success’ of a public diplomacy program be measured without jumping from visibility and participant satisfaction to a change in foreign policy preferences, and what are the ethical limits of evaluation?

Competing interpretations

Construct measures and a counterfactual to compare an evaluation focused on short-term outputs and reach with one that traces causal process, distributional effects, and unintended consequences.

Source criticism

In the USC Center on Public Diplomacy announcement ‘ACPD Report: Optimizing Engagement in Public Diplomacy,’ identify how the report is introduced and what language of results is used. This original institutional announcement is not the full report's method or evidence of a program's actual effect; the reading list provides no direct program data set.

Seminar assignment

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, draw an intervention → output → reception → preference change chain for a case from an earlier week. Drawing on Buhmann and Sommerfeldt's work on evaluation, assign one measure and one data source to each link. Add the rival explanation that relations were already improving and at least one unintended consequence; explicitly flag claims the listed sources cannot substantiate.

Synthesis

  1. Activities, outputs, technical availability or potential reach, verified exposure, reception, intermediate outcomes, decisions or behaviour and long-term impacts must be distinguished.
  2. A theory of change explains the expected causal chain and the evidence required at each stage.
  3. A single method is generally insufficient to measure the effects of public diplomacy reliably.
  4. Ethical evaluation encompasses transparency, consent, privacy, reciprocity and the risk of harm as well as accuracy.
  5. Uncertainty and null findings should not be concealed. They should form part of the evaluation and policy recommendation.

Questions for discussion

  1. What combination of evidence might provide the most persuasive measure of a public diplomacy programme's effects?
  2. Under what conditions might a campaign using open and accurate information still be ethically problematic?
  3. How can we decide whether a programme showing no outcome has failed or has been measured incorrectly?
