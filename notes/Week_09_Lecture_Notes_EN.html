Week 09 · Start from scratch

Who builds international relationships outside government?

01Main idea02Terms03Steps04Example05Practice

The main idea in a minute

Closing a communication agency does not end every international relationship. After the First World War, the Committee on Public Information (CPI) dissolved, while universities, foundations and professional associations continued working. This week, we follow a fellowship or research project to separate money, decisions and connections. Contact with government does not by itself establish government control.

Think of an everyday example

Imagine a hypothetical neighbourhood book club. A shop donates books, a school provides space, and volunteers select participants. A municipal office shares the address. That does not automatically make the club a government project. Ask who can make which decisions.

What will you learn?

  • Distinguish institutional closure from continuing relationships.
  • Require different evidence for parallel activity, facilitation and partnership.
  • Identify who sets an exchange programme's priorities.

Start with these four terms

01Private diplomacy
Organised cross-border relationships involving non-state organisations. Private does not automatically mean neutral or equal.
Example: Historical example: The Rockefeller Foundation's scientific and health connections.
02Facilitation
Helping another organisation through contacts or access, without necessarily directing its project.
Example: Hypothetical: An embassy introduces a university to a potential foreign partner.
03Formal partnership
An established agreement about the parties' responsibilities and contributions.
Example: Hypothetical: A contract allocates fellowship funding and selection responsibilities.
04Reciprocity
Partners can influence programme priorities, rather than merely participate.
Example: Hypothetical: Research changes in response to the host institution's request.

Understand it step by step

  1. 1
    Choose one project and date

    Examine a particular fellowship instead of labelling an entire foundation a state instrument. Record the organisations' purposes at the time. Later government use of the network does not prove an original secret state plan.

  2. 2
    Find the decision makers

    Who pays, selects candidates, changes the topic and can refuse? Funding is an important source of power, but does not explain every decision. Read the agreement alongside correspondence about implementation.

  3. 3
    Narrow the outcome

    Awarding a fellowship does not prove collaboration or policy change. What did participants learn? Did they use it after returning? If rejected applicants are invisible, successful alumni alone cannot establish an overall success rate.

Let's work through an example

Worked exercise: Who runs the fellowship?

A hypothetical foundation funds a laboratory fellowship. A university selects candidates. An embassy only arranges introductions. The local partner wants the research to address its region's water problem.

  1. Record funding for the foundation, selection for the university and facilitation for the embassy. Do not assign everyone the same role.
  2. Investigate whether the local request changed the budget or research plan. Attending a meeting alone does not establish decision-making power.
  3. Seek evidence of joint research after the fellowship. Do not jump from an award certificate to government policy change.

The available facts show contributions and a local request. Government control, meaningful reciprocity and lasting outcomes remain unproven.

A common mistake

Contact with government makes every activity state policy.

What to remember

One organisation may operate independently, receive facilitation or enter partnerships in different projects. Classify the relationship project by project.

Explain · examine · apply

A closer academic reading

How do non-state institutions sustain international relationships? Describing the interwar period only through the absence of a central American communication agency makes universities, foundations and professional networks disappear. This week follows individual projects to separate money, information, access and decision authority. The objective is to establish relationships through evidence rather than presume either complete independence or hidden government direction.

Read in stages

  2. Classify the government relationship project by project

01

No central agency does not mean no activity

Why this matters

Dispersed relationships can support flexibility while complicating accountability.

The Committee on Public Information’s postwar closure did not end every relationship with foreign societies. Diplomatic missions, universities, foundations and professional organisations continued cross-border work. A formal office, an organisation running programmes and connections between individuals are different levels. Extending a break at one level to all others mistakes the visible organisational chart for the entire social field.

Decentralisation has costs: objectives can diverge, work may be duplicated and oversight dispersed. Conversely, specialised institutions and local connections can provide contacts unavailable through central command. The balance is not constant. Consistency may matter most for an urgent announcement, while responsiveness to local needs matters more for research cooperation. Define success in relation to the purpose.

Compare two interpretations. One treats fragmentation as insufficient state capacity; another emphasises the resilience of numerous independent relationships. These need not contradict each other universally. The same network can be resilient in one project and slow in another. Specify the country, period and task, then explain how a particular organisational feature operated rather than assuming centralisation equals success.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical analogy: Neighbourhood cooperation

A school, shop and association organise an event without a municipal programme. Activity exists without unified command. The analogy clarifies coordination but cannot fully represent international power differences or diplomatic authority.

Pause and reason

What does absence of a central office not establish?

Show the reasoning

It does not establish an absence of international activity or expertise; different organisational levels need separate investigation.

02

Classify the government relationship project by project

Why this matters

Contact, assistance, partnership and control are different relationships.

Private diplomacy is an analytical term for organised international engagement by non-state organisations. Private legal status does not prove political neutrality, equality or insulation from government. Foundation officers can pursue their own objectives while cooperating with diplomats. Instead of giving an organisation one permanent label, reconstruct the resources and decisions within a particular project.

Parallel activity involves overlapping purposes without demonstrated joint decisions. Facilitation provides introductions or access. Formal partnership establishes contributions and responsibilities. Stronger control claims require evidence of instruction, supervision or an ability to block decisions. These are not fixed types of institution: one organisation may enter different relationships across projects or move between them over time.

The importance of funding does not justify assuming the funder decides everything. A foundation might restrict topics while a university selects candidates and a local ministry recognises qualifications. Asking who can refuse often reveals more than ceremonial sponsorship. Read authority through agreements and test implementation through correspondence. Written rules and practical bargaining need not point in the same direction.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: An embassy letter

An embassy introduces a foundation to a university; the foundation sets funding and the university selects candidates. The introduction supports facilitation. Government direction needs additional instructions or oversight records.

Pause and reason

Does government funding automatically establish complete control?

Show the reasoning

No. Examine funding conditions, decision authority, veto opportunities and practical negotiation.

03

Reciprocity is more than travel in both directions

Why this matters

Balanced mobility does not guarantee balanced control over priorities.

Reciprocity involves partners influencing purposes and resources, not merely joining activities. Travel in both directions shows mobility. If one side controls research topics, selection and budgets, decision power remains unequal. Add “whose request changed the programme?” to “who attended?” Participation and effective influence are different observations, requiring different forms of evidence.

Expert programmes often require credentials, language skills and recommendations. Administratively reasonable rules can systematically exclude some groups. Investigate applicants, rejected candidates, exceptions and objections from sending institutions rather than examining selected participants alone. Identical rules can have different consequences for applicants with different starting resources. Establish those conditions before declaring either fairness or deliberate exclusion.

Do not romanticise the local partner. A university administration does not represent every social need in its country; well-positioned institutions can redirect programmes towards themselves. Reciprocity therefore requires scrutiny of both foreign sponsorship and local representation. Examining negotiations alongside excluded groups supports a more careful equality claim than simply contrasting an external donor with a unified local community.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: Changing the research topic

Local researchers request that a health fellowship address a regional need. Minutes showing the request demonstrate participation; changes to the programme and budget provide stronger evidence of influence over priorities.

Pause and reason

What do equal participant totals fail to establish?

Show the reasoning

They do not establish equal power over priorities, budgets or selection.

04

Demonstrate the passage from learning to policy

Why this matters

A fellowship record begins an outcome chain; it does not establish the entire chain.

An exchange programme supplies resources, opportunities and relationships. An award is an output; acquiring knowledge is learning; applying it institutionally is use; contributing to a particular decision is a further outcome. Separating these stages does not diminish education. It protects real learning from being obscured by unsupported diplomatic claims. Different stages may become visible over different periods.

Identify the intermediate connection: what knowledge did participants acquire, whom did they meet, and what opportunities existed on return? Without equipment or authority, learning may remain unused. Expert networks can provide trusted crisis contacts without determining government decisions. Local interests, budgets and other institutions intervene between intellectual exchange and policy. A causal explanation must account for these conditions.

Selecting only prominent alumni hides less successful experiences. Later prominence does not establish that the programme caused it; participants may have started with considerable advantages. Include interrupted fellowships, non-returnees and participants who learned without influencing policy. Letters can describe experience richly without supplying a representative success rate. Restrict the outcome claim to the population and period the records actually cover.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: New knowledge, unchanged budget

A fellow learns a laboratory technique but lacks equipment after returning. Successful learning and absent institutional application can coexist. One overall success label would erase this distinction.

Pause and reason

Does absent application establish absent learning?

Show the reasoning

No. Learning and application differ; investigate the material or institutional obstacle between them.

05

Do not put all private institutions into one category

Why this matters

Different purposes, instruments and coordinating functions generate different research questions.

The supplied materials discuss Rockefeller through scientific, medical and public-health networks; Carnegie through international peace, law and intellectual relationships; and the Institute of International Education through exchange coordination. These distinctions prevent every activity from becoming part of one presumed diplomatic campaign. Shared international interests do not establish identical instruments, organisational roles or measures of success.

Coordination can exercise inconspicuous power. Matching applicants, verifying credentials, translating requirements and maintaining institutional directories make exchange possible. Those procedures also determine eligibility. Administrative instruments may appear merely technical while defining who can participate. Examine application forms, classifications and exception procedures alongside public objectives. An organisation’s apparently supporting role may therefore deserve central analytical attention.

Ask comparable questions of each institution: what is its purpose, whom does it reach, which instrument does it use, and how should change occur? Preserve differences in the answers. Ranking conference totals, laboratory grants and exchange placements on one success scale is misleading. Accountability can be a shared criterion while outputs require contextual interpretation. Comparison does not require making institutions identical.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: An application barrier

A form accepts only one type of diploma. The rule may accelerate administration while excluding qualified applicants from another education system. Application and appeal records are needed to assess its consequences.

Pause and reason

Why can administration constitute power?

Show the reasoning

It shapes access and determines which relationships become possible, rather than merely providing a neutral service.

06

Do not turn later use into original intention

Why this matters

Writing backwards from an outcome erases contemporary purposes and alternatives.

A scientific connection formed in the 1920s might assist a later wartime agency. This does not show it was originally created for war. To avoid treating the past as preparation for a known outcome, examine founding documents, contemporary objectives and alternatives. Explain later reuse as a distinct historical event rather than automatic continuity of intention.

The crises and international tensions of the 1930s provided a context for increased official attention to existing networks. The course’s discussion of the 1938 Division of Cultural Relations shows new coordination joining established capacity. Do not assume an instantaneous conversion of the entire private field into state ownership. Compare people, information and resources across dates to identify changing authority and surviving independence.

Distinguish prospective promises from retrospective success narratives. Applications may reveal expected purposes; later reports show how institutions wanted results represented. Correspondence and financial records can test selected success stories. Bound the final judgement by country, profession, participant cohort and years. This explains concrete relationships without declaring all private diplomacy either successful or a concealed extension of government.

Worked illustration

Hypothetical example: A reused address list

An association’s scientific directory is later used for official news distribution. Transfer demonstrates later cooperation, not the founders’ intention to enable that future use. A separate document would be needed.

Pause and reason

What does later use not establish by itself?

Show the reasoning

It does not establish the same political purpose at the outset; each period’s purposes need evidence.

Case workshop

Case workshop 1

Case 1: The fellowship’s less visible decision makers

A hypothetical foundation funds a health fellowship. A university selects candidates, an embassy facilitates visa contacts, and a local hospital requests a different research topic.

  1. Separate contributions. Funding, selection and access are different powers; embassy contact does not prove control.
  2. Record the hospital’s request. A resulting budget or programme revision supplies stronger evidence of influence over priorities.
  3. Investigate rejected candidates and eligibility rules. Visible participants’ success does not establish equal opportunity for everyone.
A defensible conclusion

The evidence may reveal different forms of power. Reciprocity depends on whether the hospital could affect decisions, not merely whether it was heard.

Case workshop 2

Case 2: The prominent alumnus

A hypothetical annual report celebrates a former exchange participant’s appointment to an important position. It omits other participants, prior advantages and alternative support.

  1. Verify the appointment and investigate the selected person’s resources before participation.
  2. Seek an intermediate record linking specific programme knowledge or contacts to the appointment. Sequence alone is not causation.
  3. Examine other participants, interrupted programmes and rival explanations. Do not calculate an impact rate with an unknown denominator.
A defensible conclusion

Individual achievement may be documented. The programme’s causal contribution and comparable effects on all participants remain unestablished.

Read a model response

The foundation funds, the university selects and the embassy facilitates. The hospital’s request is known; its influence on the decision is not. These facts do not establish government direction or lasting policy influence.

Questions worth asking

Does private status mean independence?

Legal independence differs from practical autonomy in every project. Investigate resource conditions, partners and veto rights through a specific programme rather than assigning a universal label.

Is decentralisation always more democratic?

No. Flexibility can coexist with weaker accountability and substantial agenda power for wealthy, unelected actors. Examine who can participate locally and whose objections affect decisions.

Is a fellowship unsuccessful without policy influence?

Not necessarily. It may contribute to education, research or relationships. Evaluating its stated purpose distinguishes educational value from unsupported diplomatic claims.

The academic argument

Who builds international relationships outside government?

This week examines how state institutions, foundations, universities and expert networks built the infrastructure for American engagement with foreign publics during the interwar period.

Post-war Dissolution and Continuing Relationships

The dissolution of the Committee on Public Information did not end American engagement with foreign publics. What disappeared was a central wartime coordinator, not the universities, foundations, diplomatic missions, press organisations, professional associations and personal networks through which cross-border contact already occurred. The interwar system was therefore institutionally fragmented. No single office could define priorities or provide consistent oversight, and separate organisations sometimes duplicated work or communicated incompatible messages. Yet the same fragmentation allowed experimentation, local adaptation and access to communities that might distrust an official American campaign. The analytical task is to explain how this field operated, not merely to lament the absence of one command centre.

Continuity travelled through relationships, expertise and infrastructure. Alumni remained in contact, scholars joined international associations, publishers retained distribution channels and foundation officers accumulated knowledge about institutions abroad. These resources could later assist government initiatives, but later use must not be projected backwards as original intent. A university that built scientific cooperation in the 1920s was not necessarily preparing a wartime information system. Schindler’s account preserves the purposes actors described at the time—peace, education, research, religion, commerce or professional development—while tracing how their activities contributed to a wider capacity for foreign public engagement.

The period consequently complicates the opposition between public and private. Diplomatic officials might introduce a foundation to local partners without controlling the resulting programme. A university might accept public funds while defending academic authority. A private organisation might advance a foreign-policy objective for reasons of its own. These relationships range from independent parallel activity through informal facilitation to formal partnership. They may also change as funding, personnel or political conditions shift. Calling all of them state policy erases agency; calling them wholly separate overlooks flows of money, access and information. Researchers should specify the connection by identifying who proposed, funded, selected, implemented, supervised and benefited from each activity. A productive way to study this field is to build a dated network rather than a static list of organisations. Nodes represent foundations, universities, embassies, associations and individuals; ties record a specific transfer of funds, information, introductions or personnel. The date matters because a relationship documented in 1938 should not be assumed for 1922. Researchers can then ask whether a network survived the departure of one officer, whether it depended on a single grant and whether peripheral actors gained access to more than one centre. This method also makes absence visible. A community may appear unimportant because it was excluded from archives and funding, not because it lacked relevant knowledge. Comparing successive maps reveals when crisis activated dormant connections or shifted authority toward government. Network analysis is therefore not a decorative diagram: it is a disciplined way to formulate claims about resilience, brokerage, exclusion and institutional change that can be checked against correspondence and financial records.

Foundations, Internationalism and Private Diplomacy

Schindler places the Rockefeller Foundation, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace and the Institute of International Education at the centre of interwar foreign public engagement. Together with related organisations, they formed a private institutional system for cultural and intellectual relations after the CPI disappeared. Their officers were influenced by internationalism: the belief that cooperation, knowledge and contact could reduce misunderstanding and support peace and stability. These bodies were neither ordinary government agencies nor detached charities. They chose their own programmes and claimed non-political purposes, yet their work often ran parallel to, and sometimes intersected with, broad American interests while creating transnational capacity on which diplomats sometimes relied. Private diplomacy describes this organised engagement without falsely making every grant an official instruction.

The Carnegie Endowment promoted an international order grounded in law, arbitration and informed public discussion. Its Division of Intercourse and Education circulated publications on treaties and international affairs, supported libraries and arranged exchanges among scholars, jurists and journalists. A 1929 tour brought European journalists to the United States to meet American professional counterparts, after which invitations moved in the other direction. Such programmes were intended to widen acquaintance and build networks able to interpret national positions across borders. They also selected which texts, professions and institutions deserved support. The Endowment’s peace objective therefore worked through concrete gatekeeping decisions, and its private standing did not make those decisions politically neutral.

The Rockefeller Foundation followed a different substantive route, concentrating heavily on natural and medical science, public health and the institutions that produced expert knowledge. Grants and fellowships supported scientists, physicians, health boards and educational organisations; cooperation with League of Nations bodies enabled professional exchange; journals and equipment helped reconnect European institutions weakened by war. The expected mechanism joined expertise to relationship-building: shared research and professional standards could create practical cooperation and a more stable international order. Yet scientific authority carried power. Foundation officers defined eligible fields and assessed need, while well-resourced American institutions often set standards. Knowledge exchange could be genuinely collaborative and still reproduce unequal control over priorities and resources. The phrase private diplomacy should be used as an analytical category, not as a compliment implying neutrality. Foundations possessed legal independence and could sustain work when official policy changed, but control over endowments gave unelected officers substantial agenda-setting power. Internationalism likewise contained several programmes: support for law, expert administration, scientific cooperation and interpersonal understanding did not always produce the same political choices. Students should reconstruct an organisation's theory of change from board minutes, grant memoranda and programme rules. Did it expect peace from better information, common professional standards, elite mediation or stronger institutions? They should then compare that theory with selection and spending. A programme declaring broad mutual understanding but funding only established male elites reveals a gap between universal language and distributive practice. This does not erase its achievements; it identifies who bore costs, who received opportunity and why contemporary criticism belongs in the evidence rather than outside the history.

Exchange, Expertise and the Institute of International Education

Founded in 1919 under Stephen Duggan’s leadership, the Institute of International Education became an administrative and informational hub for academic mobility. It advised foreign governments and universities seeking American partners, informed students and professors about opportunities abroad and helped manage scholarships supplied by foundations such as Carnegie and Rockefeller. The distinction between funder and implementer is important. IIE did not principally finance every exchange it facilitated; it reduced the organisational costs of matching people, institutions and awards across borders. Its accumulated information and contacts gave continuity to programmes that no federal office then administered, while making the Institute an influential gatekeeper over which opportunities became visible and practicable.

The presumed peace mechanism ran through expert networks rather than publicity alone. A fellowship or professorial visit created access; sustained work could transfer methods and produce personal trust; professional associations could maintain contact; and the resulting network might support cooperation during later political tension. Rockefeller programmes connected physicians and scientists, Carnegie initiatives connected scholars, lawyers and journalists, and IIE supplied coordination across educational institutions. Each stage remained conditional. Participants might learn without changing their home institution, expert communities might remain socially narrow, and professional agreement might not enter government decisions. Mobility and a grant record are outputs; durable collaboration, institutional use and contribution to stability require separate evidence.

Mutuality was a stated ambition, not an automatic property of private exchange. Foreign universities and governments requested programmes and local specialists could redirect imported knowledge, but American foundations controlled large pools of money and often defined merit, disciplines and administrative rules. Elite selection might create capable transnational brokers while excluding people outside established institutions. Researchers should therefore ask who proposed a project, who selected participants, whose expertise counted and whether criticism altered subsequent grants. Reciprocity becomes visible when partners can shape priorities and resources, not merely when travel occurs in both directions. Private diplomacy could widen international participation and simultaneously embed asymmetries in the expert networks it created. IIE's coordinating role illustrates how administration can itself exercise power. Maintaining directories, verifying credentials, translating requirements and matching applicants with hosts lowered transaction costs and made exchanges feasible. Yet every administrative category shaped the pool: recognised degrees, language tests, deadlines, travel documentation and definitions of a suitable institution could advantage applicants already close to elite systems. Students should distinguish a bottleneck from intentional exclusion. A rule may be administratively reasonable but still have unequal effects; the response should be measured through applicant data, exception procedures and consultation with sending institutions. Longitudinal evidence is especially valuable. It can show whether fellows returned, kept professional ties, trained colleagues or redirected knowledge to locally defined problems. Alumni prominence alone creates survivorship bias. A credible evaluation includes rejected candidates, interrupted fellowships and programmes that produced learning but no observable policy influence, thereby keeping educational value separate from exaggerated diplomatic claims.

Informal Coordination with the State

For much of the 1920s the Department of State showed limited interest in running foreign-public-engagement programmes itself, but it knew that private organisations were active. Requests concerning exchanges, educational materials or cultural contacts were often referred to the Carnegie Endowment, Rockefeller Foundation, Institute of International Education or American Red Cross. Schindler describes these organisations as chosen instruments in some contexts: they performed functions useful to foreign relations without becoming formal subdivisions of the state. Foundation leaders also maintained close relationships with officials and embassies. The record suggests consultation, referral and facilitation rather than routine governmental direction of foundation policy.

Informal cooperation took practical forms. Diplomatic posts introduced partners, transmitted information and sometimes moved foundation materials through official channels; foundations shared reports about international organisations or conditions in universities; officials drew upon private expertise while avoiding direct political commitments. The relationship could therefore be mutually enabling. It could also obscure accountability, because a programme might appear independent while benefiting from state access, or appear official although its purposes were set privately. Analysts should distinguish parallel activity, facilitation and formal partnership, then identify evidence of instruction, finance, selection and supervision. Contact between a foundation officer and a diplomat demonstrates a relationship, not central control.

Economic crisis and renewed international tension during the 1930s constrained philanthropic budgets while increasing official attention to the networks foundations had built. The essential development was not an abrupt transfer from private to public ownership. It was a gradual thickening of coordination around people, information and institutions that already possessed overseas experience. Later government agencies could recruit personnel, borrow techniques or delegate administration to IIE and other bodies, but later reuse does not prove that earlier programmes followed a hidden state plan. The interwar case is strongest when it preserves both facts: private actors sustained engagement for peace and stability, and their proximity to foreign-policy institutions made complete political insulation impossible. The expression chosen instrument requires evidence about a particular relationship, not a permanent label attached to an organisation. An embassy referral may show facilitation; a government grant with agreed deliverables may establish partnership; written instruction and supervision may support a stronger claim of control. These arrangements can coexist in the same year across different projects. Researchers should therefore code projects, not merely institutions, and identify veto points. Who could cancel funding, reject a participant, block distribution or change the report? Veto authority often reveals practical power more clearly than ceremonial sponsorship. Informality can be useful because it permits rapid consultation and protects specialist discretion, but it can also leave no clear avenue for complaint or public scrutiny. A well-supported interpretation combines official and private archives, since either side may overstate independence or influence. Where records show contact but not instruction, the conclusion should remain facilitation rather than silently graduating into evidence of a coordinated state campaign.

Mapping Authority, Access and Evidence

An authority matrix helps compare interwar arrangements without forcing them into a single category. For a foundation fellowship, cultural conference or expert-exchange project, students identify the funder, designer, selector, implementer, supervisor, intermediary and beneficiary. They then trace flows of money, information and permission. The exercise often reveals that visible delivery and effective control sit in different institutions. A university may host a programme while a foundation selects priorities; an embassy may facilitate access without paying; a local ministry may decide whose credentials are recognised. Precise mapping replaces vague references to partnership with evidence about what each partner could actually do.

The same matrix supports causal analysis. Access to a respected institution may create an opportunity for learning, while an expert network may circulate knowledge and provide trusted contacts during crisis. Neither mechanism guarantees influence. Participants can reject advice; professional communities can remain socially narrow; and expertise can be used for purposes the original sponsor did not intend. Analysts should specify the intermediate result and seek evidence at each step. A list of grants demonstrates allocation, correspondence may reveal exchange, and institutional records may show application. Policy change requires an additional link and comparison with domestic political, economic and strategic explanations.

A defensible judgement holds fragmentation’s costs and benefits together. The interwar field lacked unified strategy and accountability, but its diversity created resilience, specialist knowledge and relationships that central government could not command into existence. Private status sometimes supported credibility, though it could also hide unaccountable power. State involvement sometimes supplied continuity, though it could politicise exchange. The relevant balance varied by country, audience and purpose; fragmentation was a condition to analyse, not a verdict in itself. Schindler’s larger contribution is to make these hybrid arrangements visible as part of American statecraft without flattening them into a single apparatus. The period matters on its own terms and as a reservoir of people, practices and debates that shaped the more crowded institutional landscape of the Second World War. The authority matrix becomes more powerful when paired with an evidence ledger. For every claimed link—funding to access, access to learning, learning to sustained cooperation and cooperation to a public outcome—students enter a source, its limitations and a plausible rival explanation. A foundation report may document expenditure but select only successful cases; participant letters may reveal experience but cannot establish representative effects; a ministry record may show adoption while concealing domestic pressures that also caused it. Contradictory sources are not noise to discard. They may identify different positions within the same network or show that a programme changed over time. The final judgment should state scope: which country, profession, cohort and years does the evidence cover? Such bounded conclusions are more useful than declaring private diplomacy successful or unsuccessful in general. They also prepare students for Week 10, when wartime agencies attempted to coordinate parts of this inherited field without eliminating its dispersed authority, interests or historical inequalities.

Research seminar

Research question

Did interwar foundation and university networks engage foreign publics autonomously in the state's absence, or did they build institutional capacity later used by the state?

Competing interpretations

Test an account prioritizing civil organizations' independent agendas against one treating them as institutional infrastructure available for public goals, using dates, budgets, authority, and personnel ties; do not project a later outcome backward onto initial intent.

Source criticism

In FRUS 1938, The American Republics, volume V, document 55, code the proposed arrangements for cultural cooperation and identify on whose behalf the proposal was made. The document is not a direct record of foundations' activities in the 1920s or of an implemented program in the 1940s.

Seminar task

In a 250–400-word evidence brief, distinguish the Rockefeller Archive Center's history of the International Education Board from FRUS 1938 document 55 by source type and date. Identify two intermediate traces required to claim that capacity passed from a private organization to the state. Specify which dated records would distinguish this claim from the rival explanation that parallel activities emerged without institutional transfer.

Synthesis

  1. The dissolution of the CPI did not end engagement with foreign publics; activities continued through fragmented networks.
  2. The purposes of private organisations may intersect with state policy, but they are not identical to it.
  3. Carnegie, Rockefeller and the IIE performed different roles in funding, expertise-building and exchange coordination.
  4. Reciprocity requires local partners to be able to change programme decisions.
  5. International crises during the 1930s accelerated federal coordination of cultural relations.

Questions for discussion

  1. Does the absence of a central institution necessarily weaken public diplomacy?
  2. How can a private foundation support a foreign-policy objective without becoming an arm of the state?
  3. Which documents and indicators could demonstrate that a cultural programme was genuinely reciprocal?
